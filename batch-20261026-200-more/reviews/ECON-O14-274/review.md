# Sharp capacity and intervention bounds from observed service employment and output

The manuscript's “Unobserved spare capacity” proposition gives two observationally identical economies with different job responses. Its exact hiring rule permits a complete partial-identification result, not merely a two-economy obstruction. The result shows when capacity uncertainty actually survives the output observation, and when it disappears.

Hold the known primitives A>w>a, the workforce cap N, and all job qualification rules fixed. Let q and Q be observed baseline service employment and physical service output, generated by the source's tie rule. For 0<q<N there are two possibilities. If Q<Aq, then the capacity is point identified as K=Q. If Q=Aq, the complete compatible capacity set is the closed interval
K∈[Aq,Aq+w].
Values Q>Aq are incompatible with the technology.

To prove these claims, if Q<Aq then Q=min(Aq,K) forces K=Q. Compatibility with hiring q additionally requires A(q−1)+w<K<Aq; its lower inequality is strict because a tie selects q−1. If Q=Aq, the technology requires K≥Aq. The condition that the next worker is not hired is K≤Aq+w, including equality because of the tie rule. The previous-worker threshold A(q−1)+w is strictly smaller than Aq because w<A, so imposes no further restriction. Conversely every capacity in the claimed interval selects exactly q workers and produces Aq.

The boundary observations also have simple identified sets. If q=0 then Q=0 and K∈[0,w]. If q=N and Q<AN then K=Q, with A(N−1)+w<K<AN required for compatibility. If q=N and Q=AN, every K≥AN is observationally equivalent; larger capacity cannot create an additional service job because the worker cap already binds.

Now suppose a policy adds a known Δ≥0 to capacity, holding A and wages fixed. In the interior full-utilization case Q=Aq, the sharp set of post-policy employment counts is
{q(A,K+Δ): K∈[Aq,Aq+w]}.
In particular the smallest and largest possible counts are q(A,Aq+Δ) and q(A,Aq+w+Δ), computed using the source's strict threshold formula. Both endpoints are attained by compatible baseline economies. Since the hiring thresholds are spaced by A>w, this capacity interval crosses at most one threshold; its count set therefore consists of one integer or two consecutive integers. It does not contain omitted intermediate values. The same argument applies to baseline q=0 with interval [0,w].

A useful policy implication follows for 0≤q<N with baseline Q=Aq. Any Δ>0 permits an additional job in some compatible economy: choose the largest baseline capacity Aq+w. An additional job is guaranteed in every compatible economy exactly when Δ>w, because the smallest capacity is Aq and the next hiring inequality is strict. At Δ=w the smallest-capacity economy remains exactly at the tie and does not hire. At Δ=0 no economy changes. When baseline capacity is point identified through partial utilization, the job response is point identified as well.

For the source's A=3,w=2,q=4,Q=12 example, the full baseline capacity interval is [12,14]. Adding one unit permits q'=4 or q'=5, attained at its two endpoints. Adding exactly two units still does not guarantee a fifth job, while any increase greater than two does, absent the workforce cap.

These are model-sharp sets under an intervention that changes only K. They do not infer the resource cost of raising capacity or identify effects when productivity, wages, access or duration respond simultaneously. The catalogue's broader service-led development question remains open.
