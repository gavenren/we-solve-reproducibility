# The current quarter-power theorem and the exact square-root bounded-chief-rank class

Claim type: **Literature report**.

The available primary theorem gives two different advances toward the prompt: a full square-root gap on a bounded-chief-rank residual class, and a quarter-power criterion for **all residually soluble groups**. The quantifiers must be separated. We record the exact2025 manuscript statements and derive their consequences under the prompt's rescaling convention. No claim of a new theorem or a universal square-root gap is made.

For a finite soluble group$Q$, a chief factor$M/N$is self-centralizing when
\[
 C_{Q/N}(M/N)=M/N.
\]
It is elementary abelian, say$(C_p)^r$; call$r$its rank. Let$\mathfrak S(b)$be the finite soluble groups for which these ranks are all at most the fixed integer$b$. A group is residually$\mathfrak S(b)$if each nonidentity element survives in some quotient in that class. The same$b$must work for the entire separating family; allowing$b$to vary with the quotient would remove the essential hypothesis.

Eberhard–Maini[1, Theorem2.6] prove: if a finitely generated residually$\mathfrak S(b)$group$G$is not virtually nilpotent, then
\[
 \gamma_X(n)\succeq\exp(\sqrt n). \tag{1}
\]
Thus the prompt's implication is fully valid on this class: the strict inequality$\gamma_X\prec\exp(\sqrt n)$is incompatible with(1). The result is stronger than merely ruling out a pointwise little-o function, because it supplies the lower comparison in the exact scale relation. Since$\mathfrak S(1)$is precisely finite supersoluble groups, it includes groups residually finite-supersoluble, not only the residually nilpotent case mentioned by the earlier attempt.

The same manuscript's Theorem1.1 states a different criterion:
\[
 G\text{ finitely generated residually soluble},\qquad
 \frac{\log\gamma_X(n)}{n^{1/4}}\longrightarrow0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad G\text{ virtually nilpotent}. \tag{2}
\]
For example(2)applies to every such group with
\[
 \gamma_X(n)\le C\exp\bigl(n^{1/4}/\log(n+2)\bigr)
\]
for all sufficiently large$n$. It also applies when$\gamma_X\preceq\exp(n^\beta)$for any fixed$0<\beta<1/4$: by definition the logarithm is at most$\log C+(Cn)^\beta$, and division by$n^{1/4}$tends to0. This is a direct scale-invariant deduction, and explains why the paper states$\operatorname{Gap}(\beta)$for every$\beta<1/4$.

The endpoint wording cannot be strengthened without an extra argument. The strict growth-class relation$\gamma\prec\exp(n^{1/4})$does not, purely as a relation on functions, imply$\log\gamma(n)=o(n^{1/4})$. One may have irregular functions oscillating between very slow scales and scales comparable to$n^{1/4}$. The theorem states the actual limit hypothesis in(2); it does not assert the endpoint$\operatorname{Gap}(1/4)$in the strict-class formulation. Likewise a function$\exp(n^{1/3})$lies strictly below$\exp(\sqrt n)$but fails(2), since$n^{1/3}/n^{1/4}\to\infty$. Such a function is a boundary test, not a claimed realizable group growth function.

The finite structural input is also explicit. In[1, Theorem2.3], if$Q\in\mathfrak S(b)$then$Q^{(\rho(b))}$is nilpotent, where$\rho(b)$bounds derived length of soluble linear groups of degree$b$. Thus a fixed derived term in a residually$\mathfrak S(b)$group is residually nilpotent(Corollary2.4). The proof of(1)then uses genuine residual structure, not monotonicity/submultiplicativity of a candidate growth function. We do not replace that input by a finite list of ball counts or infer nilpotence directly from a soluble quotient.
