# TOP-1866 — A precise conditional route to the near-full counting exponent

**Claim type: Literature report.** The prompt asks whether the logarithmic counting exponent for Carmichael numbers tends to one. This report records a published conditional theorem that implies exactly that target, gives the complete criterion behind its construction, and checks the limit and quantifiers. The least-prime hypothesis remains unproved; no unconditional exponent-one conclusion is claimed.

Let $C_{\le}(X)$ count Carmichael numbers at most $X$. The prompt's strict-inequality count differs by at most one, which does not affect the logarithmic limit once the count tends to infinity.

## 1. The hypothesis and the theorem actually used

For coprime integers $a,m$ with $m\ge2$, let $P(a,m)$ be the smallest prime congruent to $a$ modulo $m$. Use the following strong least-prime hypothesis:
\[
\exists A>0\ \exists K>0\ \forall m\ge2\ \forall(a,m)=1:
\quad P(a,m)\le K m(\log m)^A.
\tag{H}
\]
The constants are uniform in both the residue and the modulus. This is the Heath-Brown conjecture as formulated in Wright [1, Section 3], with the implied constant written explicitly. Finitely many small moduli can be absorbed into $K$.

Wright's published Theorem 3.1 states, under this hypothesis,
\[
C_{\le}(X)\ge
X^{1-(2+o(1))\frac{\log\log\log\log X}{\log\log\log X}}.
\tag{1}
\]
This is a credited external theorem, not a counting proof furnished by this report. The source is Bulletin of the Australian Mathematical Society 101 (2020), 379–388, DOI 10.1017/S000497271900145X. The publisher PDF and its theorem were freshly checked.

Assuming (H), write $R(X)$ for the loss in the exponent in (1). Since $\log y/y\to0$, we have $R(X)\to0$. The trivial upper bound $C_{\le}(X)\le X$ and (1) give
\[
1-R(X)\le\frac{\log C_{\le}(X)}{\log X}\le1,
\]
for large $X$. Squeezing proves the prompt's logarithmic limit conditionally. Subtracting at most one from a count that grows as a positive power leaves this limit unchanged, so the strict-inequality convention also has the same conclusion.

## 2. The exact algebraic criterion behind the construction

A composite integer $n$ is Carmichael, in the prompt's coprime-base sense, if and only if
\[
n\text{ is square-free},\qquad p-1\mid n-1\text{ for every prime }p\mid n.
\tag{2}
\]
Here is a full proof of this classical Korselt criterion.

Suppose first that $n$ has the universal Fermat property for all units modulo $n$. If $p^2\mid n$, choose a unit $a$ congruent to $1+p$ modulo $p^2$ and congruent to $1$ modulo every other prime-power factor of $n$. The Chinese remainder theorem provides such a unit. The binomial theorem gives
\[
a^{n-1}\equiv1+(n-1)p\not\equiv1\pmod{p^2},
\]
because $n-1\equiv-1\pmod p$. This contradicts the assumed property. Thus $n$ is square-free.

For a prime divisor $p$, choose a generator of the cyclic group $(\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z)^\times$ and lift it, using CRT, to a unit modulo $n$. Its $(n-1)$st power is one, so the generator's order $p-1$ divides $n-1$.

Conversely, suppose (2) holds. Every unit $a$ satisfies $a^{p-1}=1\bmod p$ at each prime divisor. Since $p-1\mid n-1$, it follows that $a^{n-1}=1\bmod p$ at each such prime. Square-freeness and CRT give the congruence modulo $n$. This proves the equivalence. It also proves the source's all-integer version $a^n=a\bmod n$: at a prime divisor, either $p\mid a$ or the unit argument applies.

## 3. Where the analytic hypothesis enters

The elementary criterion does not by itself manufacture sufficiently many candidates. A useful general mechanism is to find many distinct primes of the form
\[
p=dk+1,\qquad d\mid L,
\]
and then choose a product $n$ of at least two of these primes with $n\equiv1\bmod kL$. Each selected prime satisfies $p-1\mid kL\mid n-1$, and the product is square-free, so (2) proves that it is Carmichael. This verification is unconditional once those primes and that subset are available.

The difficult issue is making many such products small enough to obtain (1). Wright's Sections 5–7 use the least-prime hypothesis to produce two levels of primes, control the exponent of an associated unit group, and count product-one subsequences. The hypothesis concerns availability of primes with a sufficiently small multiplier, rather than a change to criterion (2). This report does not substitute a heuristic prime distribution for those steps.

An unconditional Linnik-type bound with a fixed power $P(a,m)\ll m^L$ is not (H). For any fixed $L>1$ and fixed $A$, the ratio $m^L/(m(\log m)^A)$ is unbounded. Thus that known form cannot simply be inserted in the theorem as though it met the stated assumption. No claim that GRH supplies (H) is made.

## 4. The endpoint is a logarithmic exponent, not positive density

The statement $C(X)=X^{1-o(1)}$ means that, for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$, eventually $C(X)\ge X^{1-\varepsilon}$, together with the trivial upper bound. It does not assert $C(X)\ge cX$ for a positive constant $c$.

For example, the model function $F(X)=X\exp(-\sqrt{\log X})$ has $F(X)/X\to0$ but $\log F(X)/\log X\to1$. This is a logical comparison, not a proposed formula for Carmichael numbers. Similarly, (1) contains a subpower loss that still allows density zero.

Wright's introductory Theorems 1.3–1.4 explicitly credit unconditional lower bounds with fixed exponents to Alford–Granville–Pomerance and Harman. They do not remove (H) from Theorem 3.1. The report uses the 2020 paper's dated account and does not call that introduction a certified current-best survey.
