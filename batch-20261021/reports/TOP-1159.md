# Gauss circle error: the exact radius mean square and a density-one regime

Claim type: Literature report.

The prompt uses radius $r$, with $E(r)=N(r)-\pi r^2$. A strong unconditional average theorem is available, but it must be converted before comparison with the requested pointwise exponent. We carry out that conversion and its quantitative consequences; no pointwise square-root-plus-epsilon theorem is claimed.

Nowak's primary paper uses squared radius $x$ and $P(x)=E(\sqrt x)$. Its mean-square formula, with a conservative version of the error term it proves, is
\[
 F(X):=\int_0^X P(x)^2\,dx
 =C X^{3/2}+O(X\log^2(2X)),\qquad
 C=\frac1{3\pi^2}\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{r_2(n)^2}{n^{3/2}}>0. \tag{1}
\]
Here $r_2(n)$ counts all signed ordered representations as two squares. The paper's theorem improves the logarithmic remainder; the weaker displayed remainder avoids any need for that improvement in the deductions below. This is a credited mean-square theorem, not a result of our earlier smoothing estimate. Its series converges, for example from $r_2(n)\le4d(n)\ll_\eta n^\eta$ with fixed $\eta<1/4$, and its $n=1$ term is positive.

Substitution and integration by parts give, for $R\ge1$,
\[
 \int_0^R E(r)^2\,dr
 =\int_0^{R^2}\frac{P(x)^2}{2\sqrt x}\,dx
 =\frac{F(R^2)}{2R}
   +\frac14\int_0^{R^2}F(x)x^{-3/2}\,dx.
\]
The lower boundary is valid because $P(x)=1-\pi x$ near zero, hence $F(x)=O(x)$ and $F(x)/\sqrt x\to0$. Inserting (1), and treating the bounded initial interval separately, proves
\[
 \int_0^R E(r)^2\,dr
 =K R^2+O(R\log^2(2R)),\qquad
 K=\frac{3C}{4}
   =\frac1{4\pi^2}\sum_{n\ge1}r_2(n)^2n^{-3/2}. \tag{2}
\]
In particular the factor is $3/4$, not simply one half from the differential substitution; the integrated term contributes the missing quarter.

Subtracting (2) at $2R$ and $R$ gives
\[
 \int_R^{2R}E(r)^2\,dr=3K R^2+O(R\log^2(2R)).
 \tag{3}
\]
For fixed $\varepsilon>0$, let
$B_\varepsilon(R)=\{r\in[R,2R]:|E(r)|>r^{1/2+\varepsilon}\}$.
Since the square of this threshold is at least $R^{1+2\varepsilon}$, (3) and the integral form of Markov's inequality yield
\[
 \operatorname{meas}B_\varepsilon(R)\ll_\varepsilon R^{1-2\varepsilon}.
 \tag{4}
\]
This is a genuine near-conjectural regime: outside a relative proportion $O(R^{-2\varepsilon})$ of each dyadic interval, the desired inequality holds with constant one. Summing over dyadic intervals shows that the set of radii where it fails has natural Lebesgue density zero. For $\varepsilon\ge1/2$, apply any smaller positive epsilon and use inclusion of the bad sets. No independence of radii is assumed.

The positive constant also forces a pointwise obstruction in the opposite direction:
\[
 \limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{|E(r)|}{\sqrt r}\ge\sqrt{2K}. \tag{5}
\]
Indeed if the limsup were less than some $L<\sqrt{2K}$, then eventually $E(r)^2\le L^2r$. Integrating would give
$\int_0^R E(r)^2dr\le (L^2/2)R^2+O(1)$, contradicting (2). The same contradiction excludes a uniform estimate $O(r^a)$ for any $a<1/2$. It does not establish two separate signed limsups, since a second moment alone loses sign.

Equations(2)–(5) do not control every radius. The small exceptional set in (4) may be nonempty arbitrarily far out. Even a set of density zero can contain infinitely many points with values larger than a proposed uniform bound. Thus one cannot convert the exact mean square into the literal all-sufficiently-large-radii assertion. Nor does (5) disprove an $O_\varepsilon(r^{1/2+\varepsilon})$ estimate.

The earlier contribution proves a classical uniform $O(r^{2/3})$ bound and explains its absolute-value barrier. This report adds a different, published sharp-average regime, its exact radius normalization and both measure and limsup consequences. The source's deep mean-square proof is credited. No finite lattice scan, claim about the latest pointwise exponent, or endpoint uniform $O(r^{1/2})$ estimate is supplied.
