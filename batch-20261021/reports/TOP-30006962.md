# Geometric Picard rank at least five: a complete potential-density regime

Claim type: **Literature report**.

For every K3 surface$X$ over a number field$K$ with geometric Picard number
\[
 \rho(X_{\overline K})\ge5,
\]
there exists one finite extension$L/K$ such that$X(L)$ is Zariski dense. This is an established theorem regime, not a new general solution. We explain its lattice and field-of-definition inputs and give an explicit criterion for quartic surfaces.

Two credited geometric results supply the regime. Schütt–Shioda Proposition12.8 states that a K3 surface has an elliptic fibration, not necessarily a section, exactly when its Néron–Severi group contains a nonzero square-zero divisor. Their Corollary12.9 gives existence when the Picard number is at least5. The characteristic2and3 convention there also permits quasi-elliptic fibrations; here all fields have characteristic zero, so that qualification causes no ambiguity. Bogomolov–Tschinkel Theorem1.1 gives potential density for elliptic K3 surfaces, without requiring that the given fibration have a section of positive Mordell–Weil rank.

The lattice argument behind the threshold is precise. By the Hodge index theorem the geometric Néron–Severi lattice has signature$(1,\rho-1)$. For$\rho\ge5$, Meyer's theorem for indefinite rational quadratic forms gives a nonzero rational isotropic vector; clearing denominators gives a nonzero integral divisor class$D$ with$D^2=0$. Proposition12.8 then gives a genus-one fibration over$\overline K$. Passing from the class to the fibration uses the standard K3 linear-system/reflection theorem in that proposition; mere existence of an arbitrary effective divisor is not being substituted for a base-point-free pencil. A section is not asserted or needed.

Only finitely many coefficients define this one fibration
$X_{\overline K}\to\mathbb P^1_{\overline K}$. Adjoin them to obtain a finite extension$K_1/K$ over which the morphism is defined. Apply the arithmetic theorem over$K_1$ to get a further finite extension$L/K_1$ with dense$X(L)$. Their composite is one finite extension of$K$. This fixes the field before taking the infinite rational-point set; it does not use a separate extension for each point. No numerical bound on$[L:K]$ is supplied by this argument.

A useful concrete geometric certificate follows. Suppose a smooth quartic surface$X\subset\mathbb P^3_K$ contains four pairwise disjoint lines over$\overline K$. Such an$X$ is a K3 surface. Let$H$ be the hyperplane class and$L_1,\ldots,L_4$ the line classes. Their intersection matrix is
\[
 G=\begin{pmatrix}
 4&1&1&1&1\\
 1&-2&0&0&0\\
 1&0&-2&0&0\\
 1&0&0&-2&0\\
 1&0&0&0&-2
 \end{pmatrix},\qquad \det G=96.
\]
Here$H^2=4$, $H\cdot L_i=1$, disjointness gives$L_i\cdot L_j=0$, and adjunction gives$L_i^2=-2$ because a line is rational and the canonical class is zero. The determinant follows from the Schur complement:
$(-2)^4(4-4(-1/2))=16\cdot6$. It is nonzero, so these five classes are linearly independent. Consequently$\rho\ge5$, and the preceding theorem proves potential density. The four lines need not be rational over the original$K$.

The criterion is nonempty and explicit. On the smooth Fermat quartic
\[
 x_0^4+x_1^4+x_2^4+x_3^4=0,
\]
take the four distinct roots$a_i$ of$a^4=-1$ and lines
\[
 x_0=a_i x_1,\qquad x_2=a_i x_3.
\]
Substitution proves containment. If two such lines met, their distinct$a_i$ would force$x_1=x_0=x_3=x_2=0$, impossible in projective space. They are thus pairwise disjoint and give the determinant certificate. The conclusion is potential density, not a claim of density over$\mathbb Q$ or a bounded extension degree for all quartics.

The lattice threshold cannot be lowered by simply repeating Meyer's argument in rank4. The even lattice with quadratic form
\[
 2(7t^2-x^2-y^2-z^2)
\]
has signature$(1,3)$ and no nonzero rational isotropic vector. Clear denominators in a purported vector. If$t$ is even, reduction modulo4 forces$x,y,z$ even; divide all coordinates by2 repeatedly. One eventually has$t$ odd, when the equation
$x^2+y^2+z^2=7t^2$ is impossible modulo8, since three squares cannot sum to7 modulo8. This proves anisotropy. It is a quadratic-form boundary example, **not** a K3 counterexample to potential density or a claimed realization of that particular lattice.

The prior contribution proves product-Kummer and positive-rank-section propagation cases and explains the geometric rank-one obstruction. The added regime here covers every geometric Picard rank at least5, including fibrations for which that elementary positive-section argument is unavailable, and supplies the four-skew-lines certificate. Ranks2–4 require further geometric information, and general geometric rank1 remains outside the result. The universal potential-density statement is not proved here.
