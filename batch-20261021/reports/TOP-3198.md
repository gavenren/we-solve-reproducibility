# The stronger acyclic edge bound on every two-degenerate graph

**Claim type: Literature report.** Every finite simple 2-degenerate graph \(G\) satisfies
\[
 a'(G)\le\Delta(G)+1,
\]
where \(a'\) is the acyclic chromatic index. This is Theorem1 of Basavaraju and Chandran, not a new theorem of this report. It implies the prompt's \(\Delta+2\) bound for a broad class with overlapping cycles and unbounded clique-minor order, rather than only the cactus class treated earlier.

We state the exact external result and give complete deductions clarifying its extent and limitations.

## Exact literature input

A graph is 2-degenerate if each nonempty induced subgraph has a vertex of degree at most two, equivalently it has a deletion ordering removing such a vertex at every step. Basavaraju–Chandran, *Acyclic Edge Coloring of 2-degenerate Graphs*, arXiv0803.2433v2, Theorem1, proves \(a'(G)\le\Delta+1\) for finite simple graphs of this kind. Their proof uses recolorings to control bichromatic paths; merely applying a greedy proper edge-coloring algorithm would not establish the acyclic conclusion.

The v2 PDF is dated20October2008; the paper was published in *Journal of Graph Theory*69(1)(2012),1–27, DOI10.1002/jgt.20559. The complete recoloring proof and source algorithm are credited external input, not claimed newly proved or executed here.

## Maximum average degree below three

Define
\[
 \operatorname{mad}(G)=
 \max_{\varnothing\ne V(H)\subseteq V(G),\,H\subseteq G}
 {2|E(H)|\over |V(H)|}.
\]
If \(\operatorname{mad}(G)<3\), every nonempty subgraph has average degree below three and therefore has some vertex of degree at most two. Hence \(G\) is 2-degenerate, and
\[
 a'(G)\le\Delta+1. \tag{1}
\]
The converse is not asserted; 2-degenerate graphs can have average degree approaching four.

For a finite simple planar graph of girth at least six, every subgraph also has girth at least six. Repeatedly delete vertices of degree at most one from any nonempty subgraph \(H\). If nothing remains, \(H\) was a forest and has a vertex of degree at most one. Otherwise the remaining plane graph has minimum degree at least two. Each component with \(v\) vertices and \(e\) edges then has all face boundary lengths at least six, including repeated bridge incidences: a face boundary contains a cycle, since a finite plane graph of minimum degree two cannot have a tree component. Euler's formula and \(2e\ge6f\) give
\[
 e\le {3\over2}(v-2)<{3v\over2}.
\]
It has a vertex of degree at most two. Thus the original \(H\) also has a vertex of degree at most two (a previously deleted vertex, or one in the remaining graph if no deletion occurred). Every induced subgraph has this property, proving 2-degeneracy and (1) for all planar graphs of girth at least six.

This argument may also be phrased via the standard planar girth edge bound, with forests treated separately. No restriction on maximum degree or host order is introduced.

## Arbitrary graph structure survives one subdivision

Let \(H\) be any finite simple graph. Subdivide every edge once, introducing a distinct new vertex on each original edge, and call the resulting graph \(S(H)\). In every induced subgraph of \(S(H)\), if a subdivision vertex is present it has degree at most two; if none is present, the remaining original vertices are independent. Therefore \(S(H)\) is 2-degenerate, and
\[
 a'(S(H))\le \max\{\Delta(H),2\}+1 \tag{2}
\]
when \(H\) has at least one edge. For an edgeless \(H\), there are no edges to color.

This is not a bounded-treewidth or bounded-minor-order assertion. For every \(t\), \(S(K_t)\) contains a \(K_t\) minor. Label its original vertices \(1,\ldots,t\). For each \(i\), form a branch set consisting of \(i\) and the subdivision vertices on edges \(ij\) with \(j>i\). These branch sets are disjoint and connected. For each \(i<j\), the subdivision vertex on \(ij\) belongs to branch set \(i\) and has an edge to original vertex \(j\), giving adjacency between the branch sets. Contracting them produces \(K_t\).

Thus the credited acyclic edge theorem handles graphs retaining arbitrarily complex minor structure after subdivision. This shows why its scope is substantially broader than a block-tree construction in which all cycles are isolated from one another. Subdividing does change maximum degree and the coloring problem; (2) is not transferred back to an arbitrary acyclic coloring of \(H\).

## Why the extra one color is sometimes necessary

Every nonempty \(d\)-regular graph with \(d\ge2\) needs more than \(d\) colors in an acyclic edge-coloring. If exactly \(d\) colors properly color its edges, each vertex sees every color once, so any two color classes have degree two at every vertex. Their union is a disjoint union of cycles, contradicting acyclicity.

In particular a cycle \(C_\ell\) is 2-degenerate with \(\Delta=2\), and needs at least three colors. Three suffice: alternate two colors on the first \(\ell-1\) edges of a cyclic ordering and give the last edge a third color. Properness holds at the closure, and its only cycle has three colors. Therefore the additive one in the entire 2-degenerate theorem cannot uniformly be removed. This does not assert every 2-degenerate graph needs that color.

## Prior overlap and remaining boundary

The complete Math-1097-A1 manuscript gives a direct cactus extension and proves \(a'(K_4)=5=\Delta+2\). The present theorem extends to all 2-degenerate graphs, many of which have extensively overlapping cycles. Its deductions above identify useful recognizable classes and an unbounded-minor family.

The \(K_4\) example does not conflict with this theorem: \(K_4\) has minimum degree three and is not 2-degenerate. More generally a nonempty 3-core can block the deletion ordering. No assertion is made that arbitrary graphs admit the same \(\Delta+1\) bound, or that the full \(\Delta+2\) conjecture follows from subdividing their edges.
