# The literal short-interval assertion is classical: an analytic reconstruction

**Claim type: Literature report.** The exact prompt asks whether every sufficiently high interval
\[
(T,T+T^{1/4+\varepsilon}]
\]
contains an odd-order zero on the critical line. **This literal assertion is already a theorem of Hardy and Littlewood.** Their original paper [HL, Theorem 4.41, printed p.184] states the odd-order conclusion for every critical-line segment of length $T^{1/4+\delta}$ once $T>T_0(\delta)$. Thus the target should not be presented as an unresolved conjecture or as a new solution by this report.

The substantial scope here is a modern analytic reconstruction: we derive the stationary phase and alternating cancellation behind the $1/4$ primitive scale, identify the uniform transition estimates that must remain external, and combine precise unconditional primary estimates to prove the literal odd-order and half-open-endpoint statement. This is not a claim to reprove all contour estimates in either primary paper.

## 1. Hardy's real function and the exact zero notion

Choose a continuous real analytic phase $\vartheta(t)$ on a neighborhood of a high real interval so that
\[
Z(t)=e^{i\vartheta(t)}\zeta(\tfrac12+it)
\]
is real for real $t$. The functional equation supplies this phase; its multiplier never vanishes and has modulus one. Therefore
\[
|Z(t)|=|\zeta(\tfrac12+it)|,
\]
and the order of a real zero of $Z$ is the multiplicity of the corresponding holomorphic zero of $\zeta$.

The real analytic function $Z$ is not identically zero. Its zeros are isolated, and locally at a zero $\gamma$,
$Z(t)=(t-\gamma)^m g(t)$ with $g(\gamma)\ne0$ and $g$ real analytic. It changes sign exactly when $m$ is odd. Consequently, if an interval contains no odd-order zero, $Z$ has one sign throughout its interior, and
\[
\left|\int_I Z(t)\,dt\right|=\int_I|Z(t)|\,dt.               \tag{1}
\]
An arbitrary complex phase of $\zeta$ would not justify this real-sign argument, and existence of a critical-line zero without a multiplicity assertion would not suffice.

## 2. The analytic source of the $1/4$ scale

Ivić [I, main Theorem] proves unconditionally that for every $\eta>0$,
\[
F(U):=\int_0^U Z(t)\,dt=O_\eta(U^{1/4+\eta}).                \tag{2}
\]
The checked version is the eight-page author PDF arXiv:0907.3803v1, dated 22 July 2009, which notes corrections to the 2004 publication. We explain the central cancellation in its proof rather than treating (2) as an unexplained exponent.

For $t\asymp X$, its smoothed approximate functional equation at $k=1$ has the form
\[
Z(t)=2\sum_{n\le2\tau}
 \rho(n/\tau)n^{-1/2}\cos\Phi_n(t)+O(t^{-3/4}),\quad
\tau=\sqrt{t/(2\pi)},
\]
\[
\Phi_n(t)=t\log\frac{\sqrt{t/(2\pi)}}n-\frac t2-\frac\pi8.   \tag{3}
\]
The fixed smooth cutoff satisfies $\rho(x)+\rho(1/x)=1$, hence
$\rho(1)=1/2$. The integrated remainder on $[X,2X]$ is
$O(X^{1/4})$. By contrast, integrating the usual pointwise $O(t^{-1/4})$ unsmoothed remainder absolutely would give only $O(X^{3/4})$ and lose the desired result.

Direct differentiation gives
\[
\Phi_n'(t)=\log\frac{\sqrt{t/(2\pi)}}n,\quad
\Phi_n''(t)=\frac1{2t},\quad c_n=2\pi n^2.
\]
Thus an interior stationary point contributes, at leading order, a real part proportional to
\[
n^{-1/2}\rho(1)
 \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\Phi_n''(c_n)}}
 e^{i(\Phi_n(c_n)+\pi/4)}.
\]
The phase and amplitude simplify **exactly**:
\[
\Phi_n(c_n)+\frac\pi4=-\pi n^2+\frac\pi8,\qquad
\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\Phi_n''(c_n)}}=\sqrt{8\pi^2n^2}.
\]
Since $e^{-i\pi n^2}=(-1)^n$, the real leading saddle sum is a fixed constant times
\[
\sum_{A<n\le B}(-1)^n\sqrt n,\qquad A,B\asymp\sqrt X.        \tag{4}
\]
For any such consecutive integer range, the partial sums of $(-1)^n$ are bounded. Abel summation, or pairing consecutive terms and using
$\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n\le1/(2\sqrt n)$, proves
\[
\left|\sum_{A<n\le B}(-1)^n\sqrt n\right|\ll\sqrt B
 \ll X^{1/4}.                                               \tag{5}
\]
Taking absolute values before summing the saddle terms would instead cost $X^{3/4}$. The alternating arithmetic phase is the decisive saving.

There are two normalization details worth making explicit. First, $\rho(1)=1/2$ follows from its defining symmetry even though an intermediate sentence in [I, p.4] prints 1; the subsequent proof prints the correct $1/2$. Second, the exact Gaussian amplitude above uses the phase $\Phi_n$, whereas [I]'s stationary-phase lemma is written with phase $2\pi f$. Using $f=\Phi_n/(2\pi)$ gives the same amplitude and avoids a lost factor of $\sqrt\pi$. The bound (5) is unaffected by the fixed coefficient.

## 3. Why the interior saddle calculation is not the whole theorem

For $n$ outside the stationary range, the first derivative has a fixed sign. A distance $m$ in the integer index from a transition corresponds to $|\Phi_n'|\asymp m/\sqrt X$ near that endpoint. The weight is $n^{-1/2}\asymp X^{-1/4}$, so summing the resulting first-derivative estimates contributes at most a constant times
\[
X^{1/4}\sum_{1\le m\ll\sqrt X}\frac1m
 \ll X^{1/4}\log X,                                         \tag{6}
\]
away from the transition zones; indices much smaller than $\sqrt X$ are harmless by a fixed derivative separation.

A saddle close to an integration endpoint is different. The endpoint error in a generic stationary-phase lemma contains a minimum with a term of order $\sqrt X$. Summing that term blindly is too large. Ivić's proof explicitly isolates these transition regions and uses the particular phase, a contour deformation and Gaussian expansions to obtain the uniform bound needed in (2). Those transition estimates are **credited inputs** here, not a consequence of (5) or (6). The report derives the interior cancellation and explains the obstruction; it does not disguise an unaudited endpoint estimate as a complete fresh proof of (2).

The primary PDF contains inconsistent radius exponents in this later contour discussion; we therefore rely on its stated established theorem (2), not a purported independent line-by-line correction of that section. The exact phase and cancellation above use only unambiguous identities (3). Summing dyadic bounds with exponent $1/4+\eta>0$ then explains the passage from intervals $[X,2X]$ to the primitive bound.

## 4. The unconditional lower mass on every short interval

Ramachandra [R, introduction and Theorem 2] provides the other input. A safely sufficient specialization of its printed hypotheses is the following: for a fixed small parameter, take $T$ sufficiently large and
\[
(\log T)^{20}\le H\le T.
\]
Then, with some fixed $C>0$,
\[
\int_T^{T+H}|\zeta(\tfrac12+it)|\,dt
 \gg H(\log H)^{1/4}(\log\log H)^{-C}.                       \tag{7}
\]
This is the case $2k=1$ of the theorem's $2k\ge1$ range, with the number field $\mathbb Q$ and derivative order zero. It is unconditional. The lower bound is uniform over starting points and the specified interval lengths; a global mean-value estimate on $[0,T]$ would not replace it.

The power-20 lower restriction deliberately stays inside the actual PDF's range by fixing its small parameter at $1/20$. It is not inferred from the publisher webpage's simplified abstract. For the intervals used below this logarithmic restriction is automatic. Both (2) and (7) are genuine analytic theorems; neither is a conclusion of a finite zero computation.

## 5. Endpoint-safe deduction of the literal assertion

Fix any $\varepsilon>0$ and put
\[
\gamma=\min(\varepsilon/2,1/8),\qquad H_0=T^{1/4+\gamma}.
\]
Consider the closed interval
\[
I=[T+H_0/4,T+3H_0/4],
\]
which lies strictly inside $(T,T+T^{1/4+\varepsilon}]$ for all sufficiently large $T$. Its length is $H_0/2$, and its start is comparable to $T$. Thus (7) applies and gives
\[
\int_I|Z(t)|\,dt
 \gg H_0(\log H_0)^{1/4}(\log\log H_0)^{-C}.                 \tag{8}
\]
Using (2) at the two endpoints with $\eta=\gamma/2$ gives
\[
\left|\int_I Z(t)\,dt\right|
 \le |F(T+3H_0/4)|+|F(T+H_0/4)|
 \ll_\gamma T^{1/4+\gamma/2}.                               \tag{9}
\]
The right side of (9) is $o$ of the lower bound in (8). If $I$ contained no odd-order zero, (1) would contradict (8)–(9). Hence $I$, and therefore the exact half-open interval in the prompt, contains such a zero.

All thresholds may depend on the original fixed $\varepsilon$, as the prompt permits. The choice $\gamma\le1/8$ ensures the auxiliary interval length is less than its starting height, including when the original $\varepsilon$ is large. Moving to a strictly interior interval also prevents an odd zero at the excluded endpoint $T$ from being counted as the required one.
