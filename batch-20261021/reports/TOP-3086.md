# A rotation-multiplicity obstruction and the complete two-square case

Suppose$n^2+1$ closed unit squares cover$Q_s=[0,s]^2$ with$s>n$. At least$2n$ of them must have inclination
\[
\theta\ge\arccos(n/s),
\]
where inclination is reduced modulo square symmetries to$[0,\pi/4]$. This strengthens the earlier attempt's requirement that at least one square have coordinate projection width at least$s/n$: the new threshold has square-root rather than linear dependence on a small excess, and applies to a prescribed number of distinct squares. It is necessary, not sufficient for a cover.

Put$\delta=s/n>1$ and take the$(n+1)^2$ grid points$(i\delta,j\delta)$,$0\le i,j\le n$. A unit square contains at most two of them, regardless of rotation. Indeed its diameter is$\sqrt2$. Two contained grid points must therefore have squared integer-lattice separation at most$2/\delta^2<2$, so they are horizontal or vertical nearest neighbours. Three distinct grid vertices cannot be pairwise nearest neighbours: the grid graph is bipartite and has no triangle. This proves the asserted maximum two without any bounding-box relaxation.

If$b$ squares contain two grid points, the total number of grid incidences is at most$(n^2+1)+b$. Coverage forces that total at least$(n+1)^2$, so$b\ge2n$. Every such square contains a horizontal or vertical segment of length$\delta$. Its projections onto its own side directions have lengths$\delta\cos\theta,\delta\sin\theta$, both at most one. Thus$\cos\theta\le1/\delta$, proving the angle bound. In particular any proposed cover with fewer than$2n$ squares above that threshold is impossible. The argument permits overlaps, protrusion and boundary coincidences.

There is a complete stronger argument at$n=1$. Suppose two unit squares cover$Q_s$ with$s>1$. Each covers at most two corners, and cannot cover a diagonal pair since the diagonal length is$s\sqrt2>\sqrt2$. They therefore cover two opposite edges, which we may take as the bottom and top edges.

Consider the bottom square, containing$(0,0),(s,0)$. Reduce its angle by reflection and side interchange to$0<\theta\le\pi/4$, and write$c=\cos\theta,t=\sin\theta$. Its inequalities can be written
\[
a\le cx+ty\le a+1,\qquad b\le-tx+cy\le b+1.
\]
Containing the two base endpoints implies$a\le0$ and$b\le-ts$. Let$h_0,h_s$ be the largest heights this square covers on the two vertical sides of$Q_s$. The upper inequalities give
\[
h_0\le(1-ts)/c,\qquad h_s\le(1-cs)/t,
\quad h_0+h_s\le(c+t-s)/(ct)<s.
\]
The strict last inequality follows from
$(c+t)/(1+ct)\le1$, equivalent to$2(c+t)\le1+(c+t)^2$. Angle zero could not contain a base longer than one. For the top square, reflection in$y=s$ gives a sum of downward reaches strictly less than$s$ as well.

On each vertical side the bottom square covers an interval starting at zero and the top square an interval ending at$s$. To cover both sides their four reaches must sum to at least$2s$, contradicting the two strict bounds. Hence two squares cannot cover any$s>1$.
