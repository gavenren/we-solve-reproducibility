# Sofic graph products and amenable-edge graphs of groups, with local reduction proved

Claim type: **Literature report**.

All graph products of sofic groups are sofic. More generally, fundamental groups of graphs of groups with sofic vertex groups and amenable edge groups are sofic. These are published theorems of Ciobanu–Holt–Rees [1, Theorems1.1–1.2]. They cover unbounded graph size and genuinely mixed commuting/noncommuting relations, not only finite or amenable examples. We explain the exact constructions, the local reductions and a useful exclusion consequence for the literal question.

For a simplicial graph$\Gamma$ and groups$G_v$, the graph product is
\[
 G_\Gamma=\Bigl(*_{v\in V\Gamma}G_v\Bigr)/
 \langle\!\langle [G_v,G_w]:\{v,w\}\in E\Gamma\rangle\!\rangle.
\]
The empty graph gives a free product; the complete graph gives the restricted direct product when infinitely many vertices are allowed, because every word has finite support. It is **not** the unrestricted Cartesian product in that infinite case. For a finite induced subgraph$\Lambda$, the natural map$G_\Lambda\to G_\Gamma$ is injective: kill all vertex groups outside$\Lambda$ to get a retraction, which is well defined on each commutation relation. Therefore every finite collection of words and their products lies in one embedded finite-induced-subgraph product.

Soficity is local in the following precise sense. If every finitely generated subgroup of$G$ is sofic, take a finite testing set$F\subset G$ and apply a sofic approximation in$\langle F\rangle$ on a finite set enlarged to contain all products required by the test. Its permutation values on that finite testing set give the required approximation for$G$; extend the map outside the tested elements arbitrarily. All tested multiplication and separation conditions are preserved. Conversely, restricting a sofic approximation to a subgroup preserves those conditions. Hence the finite-graph theorem implies the arbitrary-graph assertion through the retractions above, with no uniform approximation size over all subgraphs needed.

The graph-of-groups theorem has different hypotheses. Edge maps must be injective, as in a graph of groups, and each edge group must be amenable. A spanning tree presentation builds the fundamental group by amalgamated free products along tree edges and HNN extensions along the remaining edges. Ciobanu–Holt–Rees use the known amenable-amalgamation theorem and their HNN extension argument to obtain Theorem1.2. The condition is on the **edge groups**, not on the whole vertex groups and not merely on finite intersections. It cannot be discarded to assert arbitrary amalgams of sofic groups are sofic.

For an infinite graph, any finite set of elements has reduced representatives using finitely many edges and vertex-group elements. Connect their supports by finitely many paths in a chosen spanning tree. The resulting finite connected subgraph of groups has a fundamental group injecting into the whole one by the graph-of-groups normal form theorem: a reduced nontrivial word remains reduced when the ambient graph is enlarged. Apply the finite construction and locality. This argument uses a finite subgraph **of groups with its full vertex/edge groups**, not an unsupported claim that an arbitrary finitely generated subgroup is itself a graph product with the same graph.

Consequently, a genuine counterexample to “every group is sofic” cannot both admit such a decomposition and have all the stated vertex/edge hypotheses. In the graph-product case, if the product is nonsofic then at least one vertex group is nonsofic, since closure proves one implication; conversely a nonsofic vertex group embeds by retraction, so the product is nonsofic by subgroup closure. Thus
\[
 G_\Gamma\text{ is sofic}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \text{each }G_v\text{ is sofic}.
\]
This equivalence is structural and applies to every graph, rather than to finitely many tested relators.
