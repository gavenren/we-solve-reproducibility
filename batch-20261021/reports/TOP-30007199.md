# Recognition for every Sol lattice, with finite kernels treated explicitly

Claim type: **Literature report**.

The recognition implication holds whenever the reference group $P$ is virtually a cocompact lattice in the three-dimensional Lie group $\mathrm{Sol}$. In particular it holds for every
\[
 P_M=\mathbb Z^2\rtimes_M\mathbb Z,
 \qquad M\in\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z),\quad |\operatorname{tr}M|>2,
 \tag{1}
\]
and for every finitely generated group commensurable with such a group. The deep input is the published Sol rigidity theorem of Eskin–Fisher–Whyte [1, Theorem1.1]. This is a substantive known nonnilpotent family; no arbitrary virtually polycyclic recognition theorem is claimed.

Here is the exact source input. EFW state that a finitely generated group quasi-isometric to Sol is virtually a lattice in Sol. The source's proof is developed across PartsI andII; we invoke the theorem, not a new coarse-differentiation proof. To avoid silently requiring a faithful action when using a lattice-recognition formulation, the implication needed here also follows from the weaker conclusion that, after passage to finite index and quotient by a finite normal subgroup, the group is a cocompact Sol lattice. We verify below that this weaker version already suffices.

Write Sol as $\mathbb R^2\rtimes\mathbb R$ with action $\operatorname{diag}(e^t,e^{-t})$; rescaling $t$ gives the convention used by EFW. If $\operatorname{tr}M>2$, the real eigenvalues are $\lambda,\lambda^{-1}>0$, and a real change of basis identifies $M$ with $\operatorname{diag}(\lambda,\lambda^{-1})$. The lattice $\mathbb Z^2$ in that basis and heights $\mathbb Z\log\lambda$ give a discrete subgroup of Sol isomorphic to (1). A parallelogram in the horizontal plane and a bounded height interval give a compact fundamental region, so this lattice is cocompact. If $\operatorname{tr}M<-2$, pass to the index-two subgroup with monodromy $M^2$; its eigenvalues are positive and hyperbolic. Thus (1) is at least virtually such a lattice, which is all the application requires.

The group (1) is polycyclic. Inside the normal $\mathbb Z^2$ choose a rank-one subgroup, giving the subnormal series
\[
 1\triangleleft\mathbb Z\triangleleft\mathbb Z^2\triangleleft P_M
\]
with cyclic factors. The chosen rank-one subgroup need not be $M$-invariant: a polycyclic series is subnormal, so normality is required only in its immediate successor. This avoids an unjustified integral eigenvector assumption for a hyperbolic matrix.

By the geometric group action lemma, a finitely generated group acting properly and cocompactly on a proper geodesic metric space is quasi-isometric to that space: an orbit map is coarsely surjective by cocompactness; compactness of a fixed-radius neighborhood supplies finitely many bounded orbit moves, giving the two word-distance inequalities by subdividing geodesics. Apply this to a Sol lattice. Finite-index inclusions are quasi-isometries, as bounded coset representatives change the orbit distance by a bounded amount. Hence any $H$ quasi-isometric to $P$ in our scope is quasi-isometric to Sol. EFW now gives the required lattice structure up to the harmless finite operations.

For clarity those finite operations cannot hide a counterexample to virtual polycyclicity. Suppose
\[
 1\longrightarrow F\longrightarrow E\longrightarrow Q\longrightarrow1
\]
with $F$ finite and $Q$ virtually polycyclic. Replace $E$ by the preimage of a polycyclic finite-index subgroup of $Q$. The centralizer $C_E(F)$ has finite index, since conjugation maps into the finite group $\operatorname{Aut}F$. Its kernel in $F$ is $Z(F)$, finite abelian, and its quotient is a subgroup of a polycyclic group. Subgroups and extensions of polycyclic groups are polycyclic, as follows by intersecting/lifting their cyclic subnormal series. Thus $C_E(F)$ is polycyclic and $E$ is virtually polycyclic. Passing back through finite index preserves this property. Consequently $H$ is virtually polycyclic even under the weaker finite-kernel rigidity conclusion.

The literal prompt quantifies all virtually polycyclic $P$, including much higher-dimensional solvable geometries. The argument has not established the needed rigidity theorem for those geometries.
