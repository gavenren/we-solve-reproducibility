# TOP-3367 — Submodularity, zero-defect joins, and an exact functional comparison

**Claim type: Literature report.** Schanuel's conjecture concerns all specified complex numerical tuples. This report proves the submodularity inequality for their actual numerical fields, identifies precisely where a tempting zero-defect gluing argument would assume the conjecture, and supplies a complete functional theorem for all rationally independent polynomial exponents. The functional result is kept separate from the literal numerical conjecture.

The earlier attempts already cover basis changes, the algebraic-input case, minimal counterexamples and almost-everywhere analytic independence. The additions here concern the behavior of *two intersecting rational subspaces* and a complete polynomial-family classification, rather than another basis reduction or genericity assertion.

## 1. Actual numerical predimension is submodular

For a finite-dimensional rational vector space $W\subseteq\mathbb C$, define
\[
F(W)=\mathbb Q\bigl(W,\{e^w:w\in W\}\bigr),\qquad
\delta(W)=\operatorname{trdeg}_{\mathbb Q}F(W)-\dim_{\mathbb Q}W.
\]
Although the displayed field has infinitely many listed generators, its transcendence degree is finite. For a rational basis $w_1,\ldots,w_d$, every exponential of a rational combination is algebraic over $\mathbb Q(w_1,\ldots,w_d,e^{w_1},\ldots,e^{w_d})$: clear denominators and use the exponential addition law. Conversely that finite field is a subfield of $F(W)$. Their transcendence degrees agree.

For any such $U,V$,
\[
F(U+V)=F(U)F(V),\qquad
F(U\cap V)\subseteq F(U)\cap F(V).
\]
The equality uses actual products $e^{u+v}=e^ue^v$; the inclusion need not be equality. Transcendence degree is submodular on subfields:
\[
\operatorname{trdeg}F(U)F(V)+\operatorname{trdeg}(F(U)\cap F(V))
\le\operatorname{trdeg}F(U)+\operatorname{trdeg}F(V).
\]
One justification is to choose a transcendence basis of the intersection, extend it to one for $F(V)$, and observe that adjoining the same extra basis elements to $F(U)$ makes the compositum algebraic over the resulting field. This gives the required upper bound even when the remaining extensions are algebraic rather than finite.

Combine this with the rational dimension identity for $U,V$. It yields the unconditional inequality
\[
\delta(U+V)+\delta(U\cap V)\le\delta(U)+\delta(V).
\tag{1}
\]
No transcendence conjecture has entered.

## 2. The exact missing step in zero-defect gluing

Under Schanuel, $\delta(W)\ge0$ for every finite-dimensional $W$. Therefore (1) implies:
\[
\delta(U)=\delta(V)=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\delta(U+V)=\delta(U\cap V)=0.
\tag{2}
\]
Both conclusions use nonnegativity. From (1) alone their sum is only bounded above by zero; one term could be negative.

A concrete pair makes the missing step visible. Let
\[
U=\mathbb Q\cdot1,\qquad V=\mathbb Q\cdot2\pi i.
\]
Both have dimension one and zero defect. In the first field, rational powers of $e$ are algebraic over $\mathbb Q(e)$, and $e$ is transcendental. In the second, all exponentials are roots of unity, while $2\pi i$ is transcendental. Also $U\cap V=\{0\}$, by real and imaginary parts.

Their sum has
\[
\delta(U+V)=
\operatorname{trdeg}_{\mathbb Q}\mathbb Q(e,\pi)-2.
\tag{3}
\]
Adjoining $i$ and all roots of unity is algebraic and does not change this degree. Thus proving the join conclusion (2) unconditionally even for these two zero-defect lines would already prove algebraic independence of $e$ and $\pi$. A general argument that “both pieces satisfy the expected count, so their join does too” is assuming exactly the kind of numerical independence at issue.

This is an obstruction to a proposed gluing method, not a counterexample to Schanuel. The inequality (1) remains valid in either eventual outcome of (3).

## 3. A full theorem for polynomial exponent functions

Here is a separate unconditional functional assertion, with its proof. Let $f_1(t),\ldots,f_n(t)\in\mathbb C[t]$ be rationally independent modulo constants: no nonzero rational combination is constant. Then
\[
e^{f_1(t)},\ldots,e^{f_n(t)}
\quad\text{are algebraically independent over }\mathbb C(t).
\tag{4}
\]

Suppose otherwise. Clear denominators in a polynomial relation and collect its distinct exponential monomials:
\[
\sum_{\nu\in A}P_\nu(t)e^{h_\nu(t)}=0,\qquad
h_\nu=\sum_i\nu_i f_i,\quad P_\nu\in\mathbb C[t]\setminus\{0\}.
\tag{5}
\]
For distinct indices $\nu,\mu$, the polynomial $h_\nu-h_\mu$ is nonconstant, by the independence hypothesis.

For each such difference, let $c t^d$ be its highest term. Choose one angle $\theta$ with
\[
\operatorname{Re}(c e^{id\theta})\ne0
\]
for every difference in this finite list. Only finitely many angles are excluded. Along $t=Re^{i\theta}$, each real part $\operatorname{Re}(h_\nu-h_\mu)$ then tends to either positive or negative infinity at least as fast as a positive multiple of $R$. Hence, for all sufficiently large $R$, one index $\nu_*$ has strictly largest real exponent, consistently for the whole remaining ray.

Divide (5) by $e^{h_{\nu_*}(t)}$. Every other term tends to zero, since exponential decay in a positive power of $R$ dominates polynomial growth. It follows that $P_{\nu_*}(Re^{i\theta})\to0$. A nonzero polynomial along a fixed ray either has nonzero constant magnitude or grows like a positive power of $R$; it cannot tend to zero. This contradiction proves (4).

The nonconstant polynomials generate a field over which $t$ is algebraic, so their input field has transcendence degree one. Thus
\[
\operatorname{trdeg}_{\mathbb C}
\mathbb C(f_1,\ldots,f_n,e^{f_1},\ldots,e^{f_n})=n+1.
\tag{6}
\]
The upper bound follows because the field is contained in $\mathbb C(t,e^{f_1},\ldots,e^{f_n})$; (4) gives the matching lower bound. This is the polynomial one-variable instance of the broader functional Ax–Schanuel picture.

If the modulo-constants hypothesis fails, an integer relation $\sum m_if_i=c$ gives $\prod(e^{f_i})^{m_i}=e^c$. After separating negative powers this is an algebraic relation over $\mathbb C(t)$. The hypothesis in (4) is therefore exact, not just sufficient.

## 4. Why this functional theorem leaves every chosen numerical tuple open

Fix arbitrary complex numbers $z_1,\ldots,z_n$, and take
\[
f_i(t)=z_i+t^i.
\]
The functions are rationally independent modulo constants, since their distinct highest degrees prevent any nonzero linear combination from being constant. Therefore (4) holds, however the values specialize at $t=0$ to $e^{z_i}$ and the input values to $z_i$.

For example, choose all $z_i=0$. The functional theorem still holds, but all input special values are zero and all exponential special values are one. More pertinently, a chosen independent numerical tuple can be inserted as the constants $z_i$ without changing the functional proof. That proof is valid for every such insertion and imposes no numerical degree conclusion at zero. A genuine arithmetic specialization theorem would be an additional input.
