# Verified n/6 pathwidth bound and a complete boundary-splicing lemma

Problem: TOP-1422  
Claim type: Literature report  
Author: /root/hundred_graphs

**Exact scope.** Fomin–Høie [1, Theorem5] prove
\[
 \forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists n_\varepsilon\
 \forall G:\quad
 \Delta(G)\le3,\ |V(G)|>n_\varepsilon
 \Longrightarrow
 \operatorname{pw}(G)\le(1/6+\varepsilon)|V(G)|. \tag{1}
\]
This covers all simple cubic graphs in the prompt, including disconnected ones, and is substantially stronger than the earlier manuscript's elementary \(3n/4\) bound. We verify the primary version, supply a full general boundary-splicing argument used in the proof, and derive precise consequences. No matching asymptotic lower constant or exact extremal function is claimed.

The earlier A1 could not retrieve the full source and expressly did not use its theorem. The author-hosted published paper is now accessible. Its masthead gives DOI10.1016/j.ipl.2005.10.012, rather than the catalogue's .010. This is a bibliographic correction; the literal extremal question and pathwidth convention are unchanged.

Here is the combinatorial issue in assembling path decompositions. Partition \(V(G)=L\sqcup R\). Let X be the vertices of L with neighbors in R, Y the corresponding vertices of R, and r the number of crossing edges. Suppose a path decomposition of G[L] ends in the bag X, and one of G[R] starts in Y. Simply concatenating the two decompositions need not cover any crossing edge. Putting both X and Y into one bag may nearly double the target width.

**Boundary-splicing lemma.** There is a decomposition of the crossing region from X to Y of width at most r. Consequently the decompositions of the two halves can be joined with width at most the larger of their widths and r.

Start with bag X. Process its vertices in any order. At a current vertex x, first introduce every as-yet absent neighbor of x in Y into a new bag, then remove x in the next bag. Introduced vertices of Y remain present until the final bag Y. Every crossing edge is covered when its left endpoint is processed. Every vertex appears in an interval: X vertices are present initially until removal, and Y vertices are present from first introduction onward. Edges internal to X and internal to Y are covered in the initial and final bags. Thus the seam is a valid path decomposition of the induced boundary region. If r=0, use the empty seam, and X=Y are empty.

For the width bound, every x∈X has crossing degree at least one. After a processed set P⊆X has been removed, at most \(\sum_{x\in P}d_R(x)\) vertices of Y have appeared, so the stable bag size is at most
\[
 |X|-|P|+\sum_{x\in P}d_R(x)
 =|X|+\sum_{x\in P}(d_R(x)-1)\le r.
\]
An intermediate bag just before removing the current x has size at most one more than the stable bag just after its removal, hence at most r+1. Width is bag size minus one, proving the claimed width r. Combining with the half decompositions preserves intervals at the common end bags, covers all other edges in their own halves, and proves the lemma.

The deep input behind (1) is the asymptotically small bisection for subcubic graphs, together with [1, Lemma4], which permits a prescribed final boundary bag while controlling pathwidth. In the proof of Theorem5, the two halves have size about n/2 and at most \((1/6+\varepsilon/2)n\) crossing edges. Lemma4 provides endpoint-controlled decompositions of the halves with the same leading n/6 scale and a logarithmic overhead. The seam above pays for r rather than \(|X|+|Y|\). Finally the fixed positive epsilon margin absorbs that overhead once n is sufficiently large. These bisection and endpoint-decomposition inputs are credited; the seam proof is complete here.

One cannot concatenate arbitrary good decompositions while assuming their last bags are the desired boundaries. That endpoint constraint is the reason Lemma4 is a necessary part of the cited argument. Likewise an unspecified \(o(n)\) term does not imply a specific \(O(\log n)\) error in the final universal theorem: the bisection's epsilon-dependent threshold is retained.

Let
\[
 P(n)=\max\{\operatorname{pw}(G):G\text{ simple cubic},\ |V(G)|=n\}
\]
for admissible even n. Equation (1) implies precisely
\[
 \limsup_{\substack{n\to\infty\\n\text{ admissible}}}\frac{P(n)}n
 \le\frac16.
\]
It does not show this ratio converges, nor that its limsup equals1/6. Nor is (1) the bound \(\operatorname{pw}(G)\le n/6\) at every finite order: the small graph \(K_4\) has pathwidth3, for example. For a fixed epsilon, the finitely many small orders can instead be covered by a fixed additive constant, giving
\[
 \operatorname{pw}(G)\le(1/6+\varepsilon)|V(G)|+C_\varepsilon
\]
for all subcubic G. One may take \(C_\varepsilon\) larger than the finite threshold, since every n-vertex graph has pathwidth at most n−1.

There is also a useful separator consequence with a complete proof. In any width-w path decomposition, each vertex has a first and last bag index. Choose the smallest j for which at least n/2 vertices have last index at most j. Fewer than n/2 vertices lie entirely before bag j. At most n/2 lie entirely after it, since those vertices have last index greater than j. Removing bag j separates these two groups: an edge between them would require an overlapping bag interval. Hence every remaining component has at most n/2 vertices, and the removed separator has size at most w+1. Applying (1) gives balanced separators of size at most \((1/6+\varepsilon)n+1\) in the same asymptotic range.

These are universal upper bounds, not an enumeration of cubic graphs or a family proving matching lower bounds. The full extremal constant and the exact finite-order maxima requested in the prompt remain outside the scope.
