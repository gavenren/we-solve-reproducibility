# The worst-case planar inscribed theorem and where the sphere is used

Kwan, Sauermann and Zhao's Theorem1.3 proves that every$n$-vertex polygon inscribed in a circle has extension complexity at most$24\sqrt n$. It imposes neither regular spacing nor randomness. This settles the prompt's full$d=2$ subclass. Their Theorem1.1 also gives order$\sqrt n$ asymptotically almost surely (probability$1-o(1)$) for$n$ independent uniform points on a circle, so the planar worst-case order cannot be improved to$o(\sqrt n)$.

The earlier complete exploratory attempt proves how nonnegative slack factorizations produce extensions and gives only a logarithmic face-count lower bound; it explicitly leaves the cited planar theorem as context without checking its proof. This report instead checks the planar theorem's substantive geometric mechanism and quantitative accounting. It is an attributed literature result, not a new solution in dimensions$d\ge3$.

In the primary proof, partition the polygon's consecutive edges into at most$\lceil\sqrt n\rceil$ blocks, each with at most$\lceil\sqrt n\rceil$ edges. The corresponding circle arcs contain at most$\lceil\sqrt n\rceil+1$ vertices each; their lengths can vary drastically. Order the arcs by decreasing length. An arc has at most13 earlier arcs within the source's separation radius, hence a greedy14-colouring makes arcs of the same colour well separated.

The essential use of the circle is Lemma10.2: for vertices and edge endpoints in one short arc versus a well-separated region, their slack entries satisfy a multiplicative$2\times2$ inequality. A matrix rescaling lemma and Shitov's nonnegative-rank lemma convert these inequalities into bounded-rank corrections. The source then proves, for the columns$F_c$ belonging to colour$c$,
\[
\operatorname{rank}_+ M[V,F_c]
\le8|\mathcal X_c|+\lceil\sqrt n\rceil+1.
\]
Adding these bounds over the14 column blocks gives
\[
\operatorname{rank}_+M
\le8\lceil\sqrt n\rceil+14\lceil\sqrt n\rceil+14
\le22\sqrt n+36.
\]
For$n\ge576$, this is at most$24\sqrt n$; for smaller$n$, the elementary$n$-inequality vertex-simplex extension is already smaller. The nonnegative-rank/extension theorem gives the claimed count. Ordinary rank does not substitute for any of these nonnegative corrections.

This explanation identifies why a uniform partition of the circle by equal angular length would be insufficient: arbitrary inscribed vertices can cluster, and each block must control its number of vertices rather than its angular length. It also explains why the planar theorem cannot simply be copied to higher dimensions: its decisive slack inequality uses endpoints of circle arcs, not arbitrary spherical patches.

The random lower bound and the deterministic upper theorem have different quantifiers. The former provides existence of difficult cyclic polygons and an asymptotically-almost-sure statement (probability$1-o(1)$); the latter applies to every cyclic polygon. Neither says every cyclic polygon is difficult: regular polygons may have logarithmic extensions.
