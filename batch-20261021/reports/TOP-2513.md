# Four independently conjugated soluble subgroups in arbitrary powers of A5

Claim type: **Partial result**.

We prove an absolute bound of **four**, rather than five, for every direct power $G=A_5^d$, $d\ge1$. The subgroups may be unrelated and may project diagonally across the factors; they are not assumed to be products. These groups have trivial soluble radical. This is an infinite ambient-group family, with arbitrary soluble subgroups in each member.

First work in $T=A_5$. Its prime-order classes have sizes15(involutions),20(order3),12and12(order5). The cycle-type counts prove these numbers; the24five-cycles split into two equal classes, since their centralizer in $S_5$ is cyclic of order5 and lies in $A_5$.

Every proper subgroup $L<T$ has order at most12. Indeed an index $m<5$ action on cosets would embed the simple group $A_5$ in $S_m$, an impossibility by orders; simplicity of $A_5$ is the standard elementary theorem. We need the sharper bounds
\[
 |L\cap C_2|\le5,\qquad |L\cap C_3|\le8,\qquad
 |L\cap C_{5a}|,|L\cap C_{5b}|\le2. \tag{1}
\]
Here is a direct verification without a subgroup catalogue. The possible proper subgroup orders divide60 and are at most12. For order12, the number of Sylow3-subgroups is1or4. It cannot be1: then $L$ would lie in the normalizer of its subgroup of order3, which has order6 in $A_5$. This order follows by counting the ten cyclic subgroups generated by the twenty3-cycles. Therefore there are eight elements of order3, leaving only three nonidentity elements of any other order. No order5 occurs. An order10 subgroup has its unique Sylow5-subgroup and hence at most five involutions. An order6 subgroup has a unique Sylow3-subgroup and at most three involutions. Orders1,2,3,4,5 cause no difficulty; no other divisors below13 occur. Finally, any proper subgroup containing order5 has a unique Sylow5-subgroup. Within that cyclic group its four nonidentity elements divide two-and-two between the two $A_5$ classes: the normalizer acts by inversion, and the normalizer has order10. These observations prove (1).

Let $L_1,\ldots,L_4$ be any proper subgroups. Choose independent uniform $x_i\in T$, and count prime-order elements in $\cap_iL_i^{x_i}$. For a prime-order conjugacy class $C$, the probability that a fixed $z\in C$ belongs to every conjugate is $\prod_i|L_i\cap C|/|C|$. The expected count is therefore at most
\[
15(5/15)^4+20(8/20)^4+24(2/12)^4
=\frac5{27}+\frac{64}{125}+\frac1{54}<1. \tag{2}
\]
Every nontrivial finite group contains a prime-order element. If every fourfold intersection were nontrivial, its prime-order count would always be at least1, contradicting (2). Hence some independently chosen four conjugates intersect trivially. This uses one simultaneous expectation, not a requirement that all pairs have trivial intersection.

Now let $H_1,\ldots,H_4\le A_5^d$ be soluble. For each coordinate $j$, the projection $L_{ij}$ of $H_i$ is soluble, and therefore proper in the nonsoluble simple group $A_5$. Apply the preceding result separately to the four projections in coordinate $j$, obtaining $x_{1j},\ldots,x_{4j}$. Put $x_i=(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{id})$. Any element of $\cap_iH_i^{x_i}$ projects into $\cap_iL_{ij}^{x_{ij}}=1$ for every $j$, so it is the identity. Correlations between coordinates in $H_i$ cause no problem because only the containment in every projected intersection is used.

For completeness $R(A_5^d)=1$: a soluble normal subgroup has soluble normal projection to every simple factor; each such projection is trivial, since $A_5$ is nonsoluble. A fifth subgroup can be added with any conjugator, since it cannot enlarge the already trivial intersection.

The class-count method is classical, and no priority is claimed for it. The result handles arbitrary soluble subgroups and independent conjugators on the indicated unbounded radical-free family. Groups with other simple factors, permutation extensions of the factors, and the universal five-subgroup assertion remain outside this proof.
