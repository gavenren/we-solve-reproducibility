# TOP-13 — A uniform count on each prime support, and why it is not the global abc bound

**Claim type: Literature report.** The official conjecture asks for a height bound uniform over every prime support. This report gives a precise quantitative finiteness theorem on each fixed support, with a complete reduction from primitive integer triples to a multiplicative-group equation. It then shows exactly what uniformity is absent. The deep input is Beukers–Schlickewei's published theorem; no proof of abc or adjudication of disputed proof claims is offered.

## 1. The external theorem, including its saturation hypothesis

Let $H\subseteq(\mathbb C^\times)^2$ be a finitely generated multiplicative subgroup of rank $r$, and let
\[
H^{\mathrm{div}}=\{(u,v): (u^m,v^m)\in H
\text{ for some positive integer }m\}.
\]
This is the source's $\mathbb Q$-closure. Beukers and Schlickewei [1, Theorem 1.1] prove that
\[
u+v=1,\qquad (u,v)\in H^{\mathrm{div}}
\]
has at most $2^{8r+8}$ solutions. Torsion does not contribute to $r$.

The exact theorem was freshly read in the author-hosted manuscript dated 8 January 2007. The publisher and institutional records identify the published article as Acta Arithmetica 78 (1996), 189–199, DOI 10.4064/aa-78-2-189-199. The manuscript date is not misrepresented as the publication year. The theorem is imported with attribution, not reproved here.

## 2. A complete fixed-support consequence for the literal abc triples

Fix a finite set $S$ of rational primes, $|S|=s$. Define
\[
U_S=\left\{\pm\prod_{p\in S}p^{e_p}:e_p\in\mathbb Z\right\}
\subseteq\mathbb Q^\times.
\]
Negative exponents are essential because denominators will appear. Unique factorization identifies $U_S$ modulo its sign torsion with $\mathbb Z^s$. Therefore
\[
H=U_S\times U_S
\]
has rank exactly $2s$ and is finitely generated by the pairs $(p,1),(1,p)$ and the two sign generators.

For a positive primitive triple $a+b=c$, all of whose prime factors lie in $S$, set
\[
u=a/c,\qquad v=b/c.
\]
Then $u,v\in U_S$, $u+v=1$, and $(u,v)\in H\subseteq H^{\mathrm{div}}$. The theorem therefore bounds the number of these pairs by
\[
2^{16s+8}.
\tag{1}
\]

The normalization is injective on ordered primitive positive triples. Indeed $\gcd(a,b)=1$ implies $\gcd(a,c)=\gcd(b,c)=1$. Thus $a/c$ is already a reduced positive fraction, and it uniquely determines $a$ and $c$; then $b=c-a$. Equivalently, two integer representatives of the same rational pair are common scalar multiples, and primitivity fixes the scalar. Without that normalization every one solution would yield infinitely many scaled triples.

Consequently (1) bounds the number of ordered positive coprime abc triples supported on any specified $s$ primes. The bound depends only on the number of primes, not their magnitudes or the exponents in the triple. Allowing pairs in the larger divisible closure can only enlarge the counted set, so it causes no logical mismatch in the application.

This is a nontrivial uniform **count**, despite the unrestricted heights of the potential solutions and arbitrary choices of the support. It is not a bound on the height of any one solution.

## 3. Exactly what finite-support abc follows

For each $\varepsilon>0$ and this fixed $S$, the finite family above has a finite maximum of
\[
\frac{c}{\operatorname{rad}(abc)^{1+\varepsilon}}.
\]
Choose a positive constant $C_{\varepsilon,S}$ larger than that maximum (or one if the family is empty). Then every triple on that support satisfies the strict abc inequality with this constant.

This proves the entire $\varepsilon$-family on every fixed prime support, but the constant may depend on the actual set $S$. The official statement permits dependence on $\varepsilon$ only. A uniform bound on the number of solutions is not a uniform bound on their largest height or radical-normalized ratio. Even a one-element set can contain an arbitrarily large number.

There is a concrete analogy showing why the distinction cannot be ignored. Every prime $p>2$ supplies the coprime triple $(1,p-1,p)$ on some support. These have unbounded heights, although any one chosen support is governed by (1). This is not a counterexample to abc—the radical includes $p$—but it shows that fixed-support finiteness cannot by itself produce a global height cutoff.

Nor may one take a maximum over all $s$-element supports and call it finite merely because each individual maximum is finite. There are infinitely many such supports. Formula (1) does not estimate the size of those maxima.

## 4. A broader rank formulation and a useful boundary for disproof searches

The same exact normalization applies whenever a proposed family of triples has all its pairs $(a/c,b/c)$ in the divisible closure of one explicitly specified finite-rank group $H$. At rank $r$ it contains at most $2^{8r+8}$ distinct primitive triples, even if a convenient presentation uses algebraic generators. Thus an infinite primitive family cannot live in one such fixed group.

For the original rational prime-support setting, this implies that a hypothetical sequence with unbounded abc ratio cannot use only one fixed finite collection of primes. In fact its radicals must tend to infinity along a subsequence: bounded radicals allow only finitely many possible prime supports, and the theorem makes the union of their solution sets finite. This is a necessary condition, not an existence argument or an effective search threshold.

Care is also needed with a varying group. Assigning a new rank-two group generated by the particular pair for each triple does not put the entire family inside one rank-two group. The quantified theorem fixes $H$ before counting its solutions. Changing it for each candidate forfeits the single-family bound.
