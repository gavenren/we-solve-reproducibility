# Local median orders as certificates for the second-neighborhood inequality

**Claim type: Literature report.** Every nonempty tournament satisfies the literal inequality \(|N^+(v)|\le |N^{++}(v)|\), where the second set excludes \(v\) and its first outneighbors. We give the local-median-order argument of Havet and Thomassé [1, Theorem1], with an explicit certificate and termination bound. The tournament hypothesis is essential to this proof. The preceding hosted claim about all oriented graphs is not assumed.

An order \(L=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\) has the **feedback property** if, in every interval \([i,j]\), the first vertex has at least as many outneighbors as inneighbors in the interval, and the last vertex has at least as many inneighbors as outneighbors. An order maximizing forward arcs has this property: otherwise moving the offending first vertex just after the interval, or the last just before it, strictly increases the forward-arc count.

More constructively, start with any order and repeatedly make one of these improving endpoint moves. The integer potential, the number of forward arcs, increases by at least1 and is at most \(\binom n2\). All interval endpoint comparisons can be checked in \(O(n^2)\) operations using running counts. Thus at most \(\binom n2\) improvements produce an order with the feedback property in \(O(n^4)\) time. This is a polynomial construction of a **local** median order; it does not claim to maximize forward arcs globally.

Put \(f=x_n\), \(O=N^+(f)\). An inneighbor \(x_j\to f\) is called good if there is an earlier \(x_i\in O\), \(i<j\), with \(x_i\to x_j\). Let \(G_L\) be the good vertices. Every good vertex belongs to \(N^{++}(f)\), because it is reached in two steps and, being an inneighbor in a tournament, is not a first outneighbor. We prove
\[
 |O|\le |G_L|
 \tag{1}
\]
by induction on the order length.

For \(n=1\), both sets are empty. If every inneighbor of \(f\) is good, the feedback property on the whole order gives \(|O|\le |N^-(f)|=|G_L|\). Otherwise let \(x_i\) be the **first bad inneighbor**. The suffix \(x_{i+1},\ldots,x_n\) retains the feedback property; the induction hypothesis gives at least as many suffix good vertices as suffix members of \(O\). A good vertex in the suffix order is also good in the full order.

Before \(x_i\), every vertex is either in \(O\) or is good, by the choice of the first bad vertex. Moreover \(x_i\) dominates every earlier member of \(O\): the reverse arc would make \(x_i\) good. Apply the last-endpoint feedback property to the prefix ending at \(x_i\):
\[
 \#\{\text{earlier good vertices}\}
 \ \ge d^-_{\text{prefix}}(x_i)
 \ \ge d^+_{\text{prefix}}(x_i)
 \ \ge \#\{\text{earlier members of }O\}.
\]
The first inequality holds because none of the earlier members of \(O\) enters \(x_i\), so every prefix inneighbor is good. Adding this prefix inequality to the suffix inequality proves (1). Since \(G_L\subseteq N^{++}(f)\), the required statement follows.

This gives a short verifiable mathematical certificate: an order satisfying the two endpoint inequalities on every interval, followed by its last vertex. No independence assumption about the chosen order is needed. Restriction to a suffix preserves every necessary inequality. These details prevent a common gap in choosing a maximum-indegree vertex and assuming its second neighborhood has the needed size.

The stronger source Theorem2 also gives at least two satisfactory vertices when the tournament has no vertex of outdegree0; that strengthening is credited, not reproved here. No claim is made that the endpoint-move construction or this proof settles arbitrary incomplete oriented graphs. The prior claimed full solution is not independently certified by this classical tournament argument.
