# The dense Hamilton theorem and the 2026 sublinear-expander bound

Problem: TOP-3144  
Claim type: Literature report  
Author: /root/hundred_graphs

**Exact scope.** Two checked primary theorems give substantial distinct progress on the literal Hamilton-path question. First, for every fixed \(\alpha>0\), all sufficiently large connected vertex-transitive n-vertex graphs of degree at least \(\alpha n\) have a Hamilton cycle [1, Theorem2]. Second, for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), every sufficiently large connected vertex-transitive graph of order n has a cycle of length at least \(n^{2/3-\varepsilon}\) [2, Theorem1.1]. We explain the exact quantifiers and give the full automorphism-averaging deduction for the associated path bound. Neither statement is a Hamilton-path theorem for every sparse graph.

The retained earlier A1 proves an explicit abelian Cayley traversal, basic connectivity properties and the Petersen path/cycle distinction. It mentions the2026 formulation source without verifying the new long-cycle theorem. Here the checked July2026v2 preprint is used in its precise scope, alongside the independent dense theorem.

The theorem of Christofides–Hladký–Máthé [1] has the form
\[
 \forall\alpha>0\ \exists n_0(\alpha)\
 \forall G:\quad
 n>n_0(\alpha),\ d(G)\ge\alpha n,\
 G\text{ connected and vertex-transitive}
 \Longrightarrow G\text{ has a Hamilton cycle}. \tag{1}
\]
Vertex transitivity implies regularity, so d(G) is its common degree. The theorem applies without an abelian action or Cayley presentation, and provides a polynomial-time construction in this fixed-density regime. The deep regularity/decomposition proof is credited, not reproduced here.

Deleting one edge of the Hamilton cycle gives the literal requested path. The degree condition is a fixed positive fraction of n. It cannot be applied with an arbitrary sequence \(\alpha_n\to0\) unless one also proves \(n>n_0(\alpha_n)\). In particular it gives no automatic assertion about fixed-degree graphs. Connectedness is also essential: a disjoint union of two equal complete graphs is vertex-transitive and dense but has no spanning path.

The July2026v2 preprint [2] proves
\[
 \forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N(\varepsilon)\
 \forall n\ge N(\varepsilon)\ \forall G:
 \quad G\text{ connected vertex-transitive},\ |V(G)|=n
 \Longrightarrow c(G)\ge n^{2/3-\varepsilon}, \tag{2}
\]
where c(G) is maximum cycle length. This is stronger than the earlier exponents13/21 and9/14 discussed in that paper. We do not present a superseded exponent as the current result. The source is an arXiv preprint, not a claim of an independently certified full proof.

Its intermediate Theorem1.2 concerns every connected graph, not only transitive graphs. If \(\ell\) is its maximum path length in edges and \(\operatorname{lpt}(G)\) is the minimum number of vertices meeting every longest path, it states
\[
 \operatorname{lpt}(G)\le\ell^{1/2+o(1)}. \tag{3}
\]
The sublinear-expander work establishing (3) is the external input. The passage from (3) to a long path in a transitive graph has a short complete proof.

Fix a longest path P, with \(\ell+1\) vertices, and a longest-path transversal S. Choose uniformly from the finite automorphism group of G. Every image \(\sigma(P)\) is again a longest path, and hence intersects S. By transitivity, for any fixed vertex v,
\[
 \Pr(v\in\sigma(P))=\frac{\ell+1}{n}.
\]
This follows because all n probabilities are equal and their sum is \(\ell+1\). Therefore
\[
 1\le\mathbb E|S\cap\sigma(P)|
   =\frac{|S|(\ell+1)}{n},\qquad
 n\le(\ell+1)\operatorname{lpt}(G). \tag{4}
\]
No free action or identification of vertices with automorphisms is needed; stabilizers cause no difficulty in uniform averaging.

For every fixed \(\eta>0\), equation (3) gives a threshold beyond which
\(\operatorname{lpt}(G)\le\ell^{1/2+\eta}\). Maximum path length tends to infinity along connected transitive graphs of unbounded order: equation (4), even with the trivial bound \(\operatorname{lpt}(G)\le\ell+1\) obtained by taking P's vertices as a transversal, already gives \(n\le(\ell+1)^2\), since any two longest paths in a connected graph intersect. Thus the asymptotic premise in (3) eventually holds.

Combining (3) and (4), for \(\ell\ge1\),
\[
 n\le2\ell^{3/2+\eta},\qquad
 \ell\ge(n/2)^{1/(3/2+\eta)}.
\]
Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(\eta\) sufficiently small that
\(1/(3/2+\eta)>2/3-\varepsilon/2\). Increasing the n-threshold absorbs the factor2 and gives
\(\ell\ge n^{2/3-\varepsilon}\). This establishes the path-scale deduction with its order of quantifiers. For the cycle version (2), [2] additionally uses vertex connectivity and a long-path-to-cycle theorem; that extra step is part of its credited Theorem1.1 rather than being silently assumed from a path.

A standard proof that two longest paths meet is also worth recording: if disjoint longest paths existed, take a shortest joining path in the connected graph. Its internal vertices avoid both. At each endpoint choose the longer half of its longest path; joining those halves through the connector produces a path longer than either original, a contradiction. Thus choosing the vertices of P as a transversal in the preceding argument is justified.

The exponent notation does not mean that the cycle occupies almost all vertices: even \(n^{2/3}\) is an \(n^{-1/3}\) fraction of n. Nor does the statement assert a cycle of length \(n^{2/3}\) with no loss, a common n-threshold for every \(\varepsilon\), or an explicit small threshold. The small one-vertex and two-vertex instances of the prompt have their trivial paths but need not have cycles.

The checked results supply a full solution in the dense asymptotic regime and a quantified polynomial-length guarantee for arbitrary connected transitive graphs. The gap between that guarantee and a spanning path in the unrestricted sparse regime is retained.
