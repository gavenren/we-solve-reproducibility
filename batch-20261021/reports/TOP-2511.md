# Order-divisibility transport criterion and recognition of the A5 target

Claim type: **Partial result**.

There are two results. First, the existence of the prompt's elementwise bijection can be decided exactly from the two order distributions by a finite system of capacity inequalities. This supplies a general reduction that keeps the multiplicities which a comparison of spectra alone loses. Second, the required conclusion holds for the smallest nonabelian simple target, $S=A_5$, by a group-theoretic proof rather than a database search. Nothing below asserts recognition for every simple target.

For a finite group $L$, let $a_L(m)$ be the number of elements of order exactly $m$, and let $\mathcal O_L=\{m:a_L(m)>0\}$. For $M\subseteq\mathcal O_G$, define
\[
U_S(M)=\{n\in\mathcal O_S: \text{some }m\in M\text{ divides }n\}.
\]
Assume $|G|=|S|$. The exact criterion is
\[
\boxed{\quad \sum_{m\in M}a_G(m)\ \le\ \sum_{n\in U_S(M)}a_S(n)
\quad\text{for every }M\subseteq\mathcal O_G.\quad} \tag{1}
\]
These inequalities are equivalent to existence of a bijection $f:G\to S$ with $o(x)\mid o(f(x))$. They impose no homomorphism requirement.

To prove this, make a bipartite graph with one vertex for each element of $G$ on the left and each element of $S$ on the right. Join $x$ to $y$ exactly when $o(x)\mid o(y)$. A required bijection is a perfect matching. Elements in a fixed order class on the left have identical neighborhoods. Consequently, for any subset $T\subseteq G$, if $M$ is the set of orders represented in $T$, then
\[
|T|\le\sum_{m\in M}a_G(m),\qquad
|N(T)|=\sum_{n\in U_S(M)}a_S(n).
\]
Thus (1) implies $|T|\le|N(T)|$ for every $T$. Conversely, apply that inequality to the union of all the order classes indexed by $M$ to obtain (1).

For completeness, the finite matching step needs no oracle. Take a matching with maximum cardinality. If it leaves a left vertex unmatched, explore alternating paths from that vertex, using unmatched edges left-to-right and matched edges right-to-left. An unmatched right vertex would give an augmenting path, contradicting maximality. Hence every reached right vertex is matched to a reached left vertex. Every neighbor of a reached left vertex is reached on the right, and the starting left vertex is unmatched. The reached sets would therefore satisfy $|T|>|N(T)|$, a contradiction. All left vertices are matched, and equality of the two group orders makes the matching bijective.

The large element graph can be compressed further. Use a source with an edge of capacity $a_G(m)$ to each left order $m$, edges of capacity $|G|$ from $m$ to each divisible right order $n$, and edges of capacity $a_S(n)$ from $n$ to a sink. Integral transport of total mass $|G|$ is equivalent to the matching: distribute the elements in each order class among the integral capacities. Thus a failed cut is a precise obstruction with multiplicities; success supplies an explicit order-class assignment. This is a reduction of the original condition, not a proof that it forces simplicity in general.

We now prove the asserted recognition when $S=A_5$. Its element orders and counts are
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
 m&1&2&3&5\\\hline
 a_{A_5}(m)&1&15&20&24.
\end{array} \tag{2}
\]
Indeed the nonidentity even permutations on five letters are the fifteen products of two disjoint transpositions, the $\binom53\cdot2=20$ three-cycles, and the $4!=24$ five-cycles. Together with the identity these account for all sixty elements.

Suppose the required bijection $f:G\to A_5$ exists. The preimage of the target identity must be the identity. Every other source order divides one of the primes $2,3,5$, and hence equals that prime. An element of order $p$ must map to an element of order $p$. Surjectivity therefore forces $G$ to have exactly the order distribution (2), rather than just no new orders.

Assume $1<N<G$ is normal. If $N$ contains an element of order five, it contains its whole Sylow five-subgroup and all its conjugates. These are all the Sylow five-subgroups of $G$, since they have prime order and are conjugate. Thus $N$ contains all twenty-four elements of order five, so $|N|\ge25$. If it contains an element of order three, the same reasoning gives $|N|\ge21$. As $|N|$ divides sixty, either case forces $|N|=30$. But a quotient of order two kills every element of odd order. It would follow that $N$ contains the identity and all twenty plus twenty-four odd-order elements, already forty-five elements. This contradicts $|N|=30$.

Consequently every nonidentity element of $N$ has order two. A group of exponent two is abelian, because $(ab)^2=1$ implies $ab=ba$. Thus $N$ is elementary abelian, and its order, a power of two dividing sixty, is two or four. The automorphism groups of these possibilities have orders one and six, respectively. Conjugation of a Sylow five-subgroup on $N$ must therefore be trivial. Choose a nonidentity $z\in N$ and an element $u$ of order five in $G$. They commute, so $zu$ has order ten. This contradicts (2). No such normal $N$ exists, and $G$ is simple.

This last argument does not use the classification of groups of order sixty or a list of finite simple groups. The unresolved step is the universal implication as the simple target varies. The matching criterion shows exactly which numerical inequalities a future general argument must exploit; the A5 proof additionally uses how prime-order elements behave in normal subgroups.
