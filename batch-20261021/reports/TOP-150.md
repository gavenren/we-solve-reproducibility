# TOP-150 — Cubic bounds, many independent zeros, and the fixed-degree quantifier

**Claim type: Literature report.** The prompt asks for a threshold valid for every integral form of a given odd degree, including singular forms. This report records a concrete single-form cubic upper bound and a stronger geometric fixed-degree theorem, then supplies the deductions that can safely be made from them. It does not turn polynomial dependence on the number of equations into a polynomial bound in their degree.

Let $\nu(d)$ be the least integer such that every integral degree-$d$ form in $n>\nu(d)$ variables has a nonzero integral zero. A rational zero and an integral zero are equivalent here: multiply a nonzero rational vector by a common denominator and use homogeneity.

## 1. A genuine single-form upper bound

Heath-Brown's published theorem [1, Theorem 1] states that every integral cubic form in at least fourteen variables has a nonzero integral zero. No nonsingularity or local-solvability assumption is present in that statement. Therefore
\[
\nu(3)\le13.
\tag{1}
\]
The shift by one comes from the prompt's strict inequality $n>\nu(3)$.

The earlier archive contribution proves a nine-variable cubic local obstruction, as a special case of its degree-$d$ norm-form obstruction in $d^2$ variables. Its proof was read: irreducibility modulo a prime makes each nonzero block norm have valuation divisible by $d$; the weights $1,p,\ldots,p^{d-1}$ give a unique least valuation. Thus its construction gives $\nu(3)\ge9$. Combining that already-established lower obstruction with the credited upper theorem yields the rigorous bracket
\[
9\le\nu(3)\le13.
\tag{2}
\]
The lower proof is credited to the earlier contribution here rather than submitted again as new content. Neither inequality asserts that an intermediate value is optimal.

## 2. What the newer polynomial theorem says

Lampert, Snowden and Ziegler [2, Theorem 1.1] consider a number field $K$, and $s$ forms of odd degrees at most a fixed $d$. If $Z\subseteq\mathbb A^n$ is their common zero set, the Zariski closure of $Z(K)$ satisfies
\[
\operatorname{codim}_{\mathbb A^n}\overline{Z(K)}<A(d)s^{B(d)}.
\tag{3}
\]
In particular their universal existence threshold is at most $A(d)s^{B(d)}$. The checked source is arXiv:2512.00697v1, posted 30 November 2025. It is a credited preprint result, not a new proof or a claimed published theorem.

Both constants depend on the degree. For a single form, setting $s=1$ leaves an upper bound involving $A(d)$, whose growth is not specified by the phrase “polynomial in $s$”. For instance, a family of constants $A(d)=2^{2^d}$ would still be compatible with that phrase. That is an illustration of quantifier order, not the authors' asserted formula.

The source's extension to even degrees assumes a totally imaginary number field (or its stated Brauer-field hypotheses). It cannot be applied to arbitrary even-degree forms over $\mathbb Q$: the sum of squares is an immediate obstruction. Our application remains $K=\mathbb Q$ and odd degrees.

## 3. A stronger consequence than existence of one zero

Write $C=A(d)s^{B(d)}$. When $n>C$, (3) implies that the linear span over $K$ of the rational zeros has dimension greater than $n-C$. Indeed, let $W$ be that span. Every rational zero lies in the linear subspace $W$, and $W$ is Zariski closed. Therefore
\[
\overline{Z(K)}\subseteq W,\qquad
\dim W\ge\dim\overline{Z(K)}>n-C.
\tag{4}
\]
In particular $Z(K)$ contains at least $\lfloor n-C\rfloor+1$ linearly independent vectors whenever that integer is positive.

To justify the last assertion without replacing the set by its closure, choose rational zeros successively. If fewer than $\dim W$ chosen zeros span all remaining ones, their span would equal $W$, contradicting its dimension. Thus a basis of $W$ can be selected from actual zeros. For $K=\mathbb Q$, clearing each vector's denominators separately preserves both its zero property and linear independence, producing integral zero vectors.

This is a useful consequence for the literal unrestricted-form problem. It gives many independent solutions in a sufficiently large ambient space and avoids a hidden assumption of smoothness. It does not say that their entire linear span consists of zeros: sums of zeros of a nonlinear form need not be zeros.

There is also a version with prescribed homogeneous linear constraints. Let $V\subseteq\mathbb Q^n$ be a rational linear subspace of codimension $r$. Choose a rational basis of $V$ and restrict the original forms to it. The restricted polynomials are either zero or homogeneous of the same odd degrees. Apply the existence conclusion with at most $s$ nonzero forms in $n-r$ variables. If $n-r>C$, a nonzero rational point in $V$ annihilates all the forms. Clear denominators to obtain an integral point. No primitivity, height bound, congruence constraint or avoidance of arbitrary hyperplanes is inferred.
