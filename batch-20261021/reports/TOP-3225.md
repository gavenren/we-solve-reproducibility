# Strong coloring for arbitrary small parts: a rigorous dense-range conversion

**Claim type: Literature report.** For a finite simple graph \(G\) of maximum degree \(\Delta\ge1\) and order \(n\le4\Delta\), **every** partition into parts of size at most \(2\Delta\) admits the strong coloring requested in the prompt.

The proof uses a credited theorem of Axenovich and Martin, with a nontrivial conversion between their padded equal-part convention and the literal prompt's arbitrary-small-part convention. This conversion is essential: the cited theorem's numerical \(n\le6\Delta\) range cannot simply be copied without addressing which partitions it covers.

## The checked theorem and its three-part consequence

Axenovich–Martin, *On the strong chromatic number of graphs*, arXiv1605.06574v2, Theorem1.1, proves strong chromatic number at most \(2\Delta\) when \(\Delta\ge n/6\), under the conventional padding to \(\lceil n/(2\Delta)\rceil\) equal parts. Their proof explicitly constructs a strong \(2\Delta\)-coloring for every partition into three equal parts of size \(2\Delta\) when the graph has \(6\Delta\) vertices and maximum degree at most \(\Delta\). The one- and two-part regimes are also covered.

Equivalently, in the three-part case the tripartite complement has a triangle factor; the source states that equivalent formulation as Corollary3.1. This is the external theorem. Its multi-case recoloring proof is credited, not claimed reproduced in full.

## Coarsening arbitrary small parts into at most three full blocks

We prove a general packing lemma. Suppose nonempty disjoint parts have sizes at most \(r\), and total size at most \(2r\). Repeatedly merge two parts whenever their sum is at most \(r\). This process terminates, preserving the size bound.

There can be at most three terminal parts. If there were at least four, choose four of them. No two can be merged, so the sum of the first two is greater than \(r\), and the sum of the other two is greater than \(r\). Those four alone would have total size greater than \(2r\), a contradiction. No assertion that they can always be merged into two parts is needed.

Apply this to the given partition of \(G\) with \(r=2\Delta\) and \(n\le4\Delta=2r\). Obtain at most three groups, each a union of whole original parts and of size at most \(r\). Add isolated vertices to make each group have size exactly \(r\), creating additional all-isolated groups if fewer than three were obtained. The resulting graph \(G^+\) has \(3r=6\Delta\) vertices and maximum degree exactly \(\Delta\), because the original maximum-degree vertex remains.

The credited three-part theorem gives a proper \(r\)-coloring of \(G^+\) that is injective on each full group. Restrict it to \(G\). Each original part lies within one such group, so injectivity holds there too. Original graph edges are unchanged. This proves the stated literal-protocol result.

Neither the number of original small parts nor divisibility of \(n\) is restricted. Extra edges that a coarsening would impose are harmless, because its strong coloring also satisfies the weaker original partition constraints.

## An elementary check of the two-block regime

For two full parts \(A,B\) each of size \(r=2\Delta\), form the bipartite graph whose edges are **nonedges of \(G\)** between the parts. Its minimum degree is at least \(r-\Delta=r/2\).

It has a perfect matching. Indeed for \(S\subseteq A\) with \(|S|\le r/2\), its neighborhood has size at least \(r/2\ge|S|\) unless \(S\) is empty, which is trivial. If \(|S|>r/2\) and some \(b\in B\) lay outside its neighborhood, every neighbor of \(b\) in this bipartite nonedge graph would lie in \(A-S\), whose size is less than \(r/2\), contradicting minimum degree. Thus \(N(S)=B\) for these larger sets. Hall's condition follows.

Give each matched nonedge pair its own color. Every color appears once per full part, and its only two vertices are nonadjacent in \(G\), so the coloring is proper even when \(G\) has edges inside the parts. This verifies the easy base of the source reduction; the three-block triangle-factor theorem is the substantive external step.

## Why a careless six-Delta statement is not justified here

Under the prompt, a partition may have many small parts. Adding isolated vertices to **each** part can give far more than the minimal number of full parts used in the source definition. A density bound on the original \(n\) alone does not control that padded order.

For example, with \(r=4\), four parts of size three have total order \(12=3r\), but no two parts can be merged into a block of size four. They require four blocks if each is to remain contained in one block. This is a counterexample to that proposed coarsening step, not a counterexample to strong colorability itself.

Our \(n\le2r\) lemma genuinely guarantees at most three blocks, so the application above is valid. No assertion that the literal protocol fails at larger \(n\), or that \(4\Delta\) is its optimal density boundary, is made.

## Sharp palette obstruction and prior overlap

For every \(\Delta\ge1\), consider \(K_{\Delta,\Delta}\) with shores \(A,B\). Use the partition \(A,B\), whose part sizes are at most \(2\Delta-1\). A strong \((2\Delta-1)\)-coloring would have to use \(\Delta\) distinct colors on each shore, while no color could appear on both shores because all cross-pairs are adjacent. It would need \(2\Delta\) colors, a contradiction. Thus a universal palette smaller than \(2\Delta\) cannot work even in this dense family.

The present contribution addresses arbitrary graph structure in a precisely justified density range, and supplies the missing translation to the literal unequal-part convention. It does not merely repeat the cited \(6\Delta\) number.
