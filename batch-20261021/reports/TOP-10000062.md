# Periodicity for the equilateral subclass in both geometries

Suppose every triangle of the given geometric triangulation is a congruent equilateral geodesic triangle of positive side length$a$. Then the triangulation is periodic in the prompt's cocompact discrete-group sense. This result is independent of the radius of the local comparison balls; it proves a substantial rigid subclass but does not infer equal-sided triangles from the prompt's local homogeneity assumption.

Let$\alpha$ be the angle of one triangle. At every vertex the incident angles add to$2\pi$, because the underlying ambient metric has no cone singularity. Local finiteness and positive triangle angles give a finite integer degree$q$ satisfying$q\alpha=2\pi$. Thus the degree is constant. In the Euclidean plane$\alpha=\pi/3$ and$q=6$. In the hyperbolic plane$0<\alpha<\pi/3$, so$q\ge7$. No arbitrary hyperbolic edge length can occur: the common equilateral angle must be$2\pi/q$.

The key geometric rigidity is that a geodesic edge has exactly one equilateral triangle of side$a$ on either side. In Euclidean geometry these are the two intersections of circles of radius$a$ about its endpoints. The same statement holds in the hyperbolic plane, by reflection symmetry and the hyperbolic cosine law. Consequently any one triangle determines its neighboring triangle across each edge, and iterating determines the entire triangulation. The dual adjacency graph is connected: a path between interior points of two triangles can be perturbed to avoid vertices, and its compact image meets finitely many triangles by local finiteness. It crosses a finite sequence of adjacent triangles.

This gives a useful uniqueness statement without assuming a global combinatorial regularity theorem. If two such triangulations share one triangle, they share all its neighbors by the preceding geometric uniqueness. Induction along the connected dual graph shows that they coincide everywhere.

Now reflect the entire plane in the geodesic line of any edge of the triangulation. The reflected complex is another locally finite triangulation by the same equilateral triangles. It shares the two triangles incident to that edge with the original, exchanged by the reflection. The uniqueness statement forces the reflected complex to coincide with the original. Thus every triangle-edge reflection is a global ambient symmetry, not just a local symmetry of a star.

Fix a triangle$\tau$ and let$\Gamma$ be the group generated by reflections in its three sides. It acts transitively on triangles. Indeed if$g\tau$ is reached, reflection in one of its sides is the conjugate by$g$ of a side reflection of$\tau$, and reaches every neighboring triangle. Connectedness again reaches the whole complex. A compact triangle therefore has translates covering the plane, giving cocompactness.

For discreteness, take an interior point$z$ of$\tau$. A sufficiently small neighborhood of the identity in the ambient isometry group sends$z$ inside$\tau$. Any symmetry this close to the identity sends$\tau$ to a triangle having interior intersection with$\tau$, hence to$\tau$ itself. Its possible restrictions then lie in the finite isometry group of an equilateral triangle; by shrinking that identity neighborhood further only the identity remains. Thus the identity is isolated and$\Gamma$ is discrete. This argument avoids treating the whole equilateral triangle as a fundamental reflection chamber when$q$ is odd; its stabilizer need not be trivial.

In the Euclidean case propagation gives the usual triangular lattice, with two independent translations. In the hyperbolic case it gives the regular triangular tiling of degree$q$, and the compact-triangle orbit proves the required cocompactness directly. No claim is made about triangulations with different side lengths or shapes, even if their vertex-centered balls are isometric. Those are precisely the extra degrees of freedom absent from the propagation proof.
