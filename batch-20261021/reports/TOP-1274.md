# A published subconvexity input and uniform bounds for every fixed zeta derivative

Claim type: **Literature report**.

The prompt requires a pointwise bound with every positive exponent. Bourgain's published theorem gives the unconditional exponent $\theta=13/84$, with an arbitrarily small positive loss. We record its exact scope and prove a uniform consequence for all fixed derivatives throughout the critical strip:
\[
 |\zeta^{(j)}(\sigma+it)|\ll_{j,\varepsilon}
 (|t|+2)^{B(\sigma)+\varepsilon},\qquad 0\le\sigma\le1,\quad |t|\ge2,
 \tag{1}
\]
where
\[
B(\sigma)=
\begin{cases}
\frac12-\frac{29}{42}\sigma,&0\le\sigma\le\frac12,\\
\frac{13}{42}(1-\sigma),&\frac12\le\sigma\le1.
\end{cases} \tag{2}
\]
The constant is uniform in $\sigma$. The same argument shows that the prompt's Lindelöf hypothesis would replace (2) by $\max(0,\frac12-\sigma)$ for every fixed derivative. These analytic consequences are classical in mechanism and are not claimed novel. They specify what a critical-line estimate actually controls, including derivatives; they do not replace the requested exponent zero by a positive exponent.

**The credited input.** Theorem5 of Bourgain, arXiv:1408.5794v2, states
\[
|\zeta(\tfrac12+it)|\ll_\eta |t|^{13/84+\eta}
\quad\text{for every fixed }\eta>0.
\tag{3}
\]
The paper was published in JAMS30 (2017),205–224. Its proof uses a decoupling estimate in the Bombieri–Iwaniec exponential-sum method and the approximate functional equation. We use (3) as a deep theorem, not as a theorem proved in this contribution. In particular, a mean-value estimate for an auxiliary exponential sum is not being asserted directly to be a pointwise zeta bound. We make no claim that this is the latest exponent available on the date of this report.

**Interpolation with the pole removed.** We give the analytic argument to prevent an endpoint or uniformity assumption from entering unnoticed. Put
\[
F(s)=\frac{s-1}{s+2}\zeta(s).
\]
It is holomorphic in the closed strip $\frac12\le\Re s\le1+\delta$, for any fixed small $\delta>0$, including at $s=1$. It has polynomial growth there, for example by Euler summation and continuation. On the right boundary its modulus is bounded, by absolute convergence; on the left it is $O_\eta((|t|+2)^{\theta+\eta})$.

Here is a localized maximum-principle proof of the needed convexity step. For fixed $t_0$ with $T=|t_0|+2$, let $L(s)$ be the real-affine function of $s$ taking values $\theta+\eta$ at $s=1/2$ and zero at $s=1+\delta$. Apply the maximum principle to
\[
F(s)T^{-L(s)}\exp((s-it_0)^2).
\]
On either vertical boundary the Gaussian factor dominates the polynomial dependence on the boundary height, giving a bound independent of $t_0$. On horizontal edges at heights tending to either infinity it tends to zero, since $F$ has polynomial growth and $T$ is fixed in this application. Letting those edges tend to infinity therefore gives
\[
|\zeta(\sigma+it_0)|
 \ll_{\delta,\eta}T^{(\theta+\eta)(1+\delta-\sigma)/(1/2+\delta)}
\quad(1/2\le\sigma\le1+\delta,\ |t_0|\ge2). \tag{4}
\]
The rational factor relating $F$ and $\zeta$ is uniformly bounded above and below at these heights. Choosing $\delta,\eta$ sufficiently small in terms of $\varepsilon$ makes the exponent in (4) at most $2\theta(1-\sigma)+\varepsilon$, uniformly for $1/2\le\sigma\le1$. No bound for $\zeta(1)$ was used.

For $0\le\sigma\le1/2$, the functional equation and Stirling's formula give, uniformly on that interval at large height,
\[
|\zeta(\sigma+it)|
 \ll (|t|+2)^{1/2-\sigma}|\zeta(1-\sigma-it)|.
\]
Substitution of the right-half estimate yields the first branch of (2). In particular its endpoint values are $1/2,\theta,0$ at $\sigma=0,1/2,1$.

**Cauchy estimates and all derivatives.** Fix $j$ and $\varepsilon$. Choose a constant radius $r>0$ sufficiently small in terms of $\varepsilon$, independently of $t,\sigma$. Estimates (4), the functional equation and absolute convergence also give the corresponding estimates on a slightly enlarged strip $-r\le\Re s\le1+r$. Extend $B$ to the left by $1/2-\sigma$ and to the right by zero; the small extension and any interpolation losses can be made $O(r)+\varepsilon/4$. On a circle of radius $r$ about $\sigma+it$, its maximal exponent is at most $B(\sigma)+O(r)+\varepsilon/2$, since this piecewise linear function is Lipschitz. All these disks are far from the pole once $|t|$ is large. Cauchy's formula gives
\[
|\zeta^{(j)}(\sigma+it)|
 \le j!r^{-j}\max_{|w-(\sigma+it)|=r}|\zeta(w)|,
\]
which proves (1) after reducing $r$ and the intermediate losses. The compact range of remaining heights is harmless. The constant may depend on $j,\varepsilon$; this is not a bound uniform as $j$ tends to infinity.

If Lindelöf is assumed, repeat the argument with arbitrary critical-line exponent $\eta$ instead of $\theta+\eta$. This proves the stated derivative bound with exponent $\max(0,1/2-\sigma)+\varepsilon$. Conversely its case $j=0,\sigma=1/2$ is exactly the literal prompt. Thus uniform derivative estimates do not provide an easier independent proof of Lindelöf.

The two earlier contributions develop the all-fixed-moments equivalence and smooth-peak countermodels. The present report supplies a different complete analytic consequence of a published pointwise theorem and checks its pole, strip, derivative and quantifier boundaries. It obtains neither a new subconvexity exponent nor Lindelöf. A sequence of estimates with exponents tending to zero would require actually proved inputs with those exponents; a single positive $\theta$, even for every fixed derivative, does not supply them.
