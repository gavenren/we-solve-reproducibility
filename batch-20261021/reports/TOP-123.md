# Two small primes modulo a prime: exact published coverage and the binary obstruction

Claim type: Literature report.

For $G=(\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z)^\times$ and $A=\{q\bmod p:q<p,\ q\text{ prime}\}$ the target is $A^2=G$ for every sufficiently large prime $p$. The following unconditional theorem regime is due to Matomäki and Teräväinen, not to this report:
\[
 |A^2|\ge(11/16-\varepsilon)(p-1)
 \quad(p\ge p_0(\varepsilon)),\qquad A^3=G
 \quad(p\ge p_1).
 \tag{1}
\]
The first is their Theorem1.3(ii), since a prime modulus is cube-free; the second is Theorem1.1(i). The constants are uniform in the requested residue, with thresholds as stated. Repeated primes are permitted. Their notation allows primes at most the modulus, but a factor equal to $p$ cannot contribute to a reduced residue, so their statements imply exactly (1) with primes strictly below $p$.

This improves a mere existence statement in two distinct directions: more than eleven sixteenths of all nonzero targets have a binary representation, while every target has a ternary representation. Neither conclusion removes the last factor from the ternary statement. These are credited published results, not claimed current optimal records.

Here are exact elementary consequences and obstruction calculations that make that gap concrete. If $B\subset G$ has $|B|>|G|/2$, then $BB=G$. For each $a\in G$, the sets $B$ and $aB^{-1}$ have sizes summing to more than $|G|$, so some $b=ac^{-1}$ belongs to their intersection, giving $a=bc$ with $b,c\in B$. Take $B=A^2$ and fix $\varepsilon<3/16$ in (1). This proves $A^4=G$ by a short argument. It does **not** prove $A^2=G$: the half-density hypothesis applies to $A^2$, whereas $|A|=\pi(p-1)$ has density tending to zero in $G$. Applying the intersection argument to the wrong set would lose precisely the distinction under study.

The same reasoning has a representation-count form. For $B=A^2$ define
\[
 M_B(a)=|\{(b,c)\in B^2:bc=a\}|.
\]
Since $M_B(a)=|B\cap aB^{-1}|$, inclusion-exclusion and (1) imply
\[
 M_B(a)\ge 2|B|-|G|
 \ge(3/8-2\varepsilon)(p-1)
\]
uniformly in $a$. Choosing one prime-pair representative for each element of $B$ turns these into at least that many distinct ordered four-prime representations: different $(b,c)$ give different selected quadruples. This lower count concerns four factors, not the two-factor conjecture.

For the binary problem itself, put
\[
 R(a)=|\{(q_1,q_2):q_i<p\text{ prime},\ q_1q_2\equiv a\bmod p\}|.
\]
Character orthogonality gives the exact identity
\[
 R(a)=\frac1{p-1}\sum_{\chi\bmod p}
 \overline{\chi(a)}\left(\sum_{q<p\ {\rm prime}}\chi(q)\right)^2.
 \tag{2}
\]
The square is an algebraic square, not an absolute square. Consequently arbitrary bounds on the total energy do not automatically give positivity of every $R(a)$. For example, the immediate triangle-inequality sufficient condition would require
\[
 \sum_{\chi\ne\chi_0}\left|\sum_{q<p}\chi(q)\right|^2
 <\pi(p-1)^2.
\]
But Parseval makes the left side equal to $(p-1)\pi(p-1)-\pi(p-1)^2$, because all those primes have distinct nonzero residues. Thus this crude sufficient condition is equivalent to $|A|>(p-1)/2$, which fails for large $p$. It is therefore not a viable route from orthogonality alone to the desired full binary coverage. The analytic theorem must preserve more structure than this loss by absolute values.

There is also a useful parity-of-cosets diagnostic. Let $\psi$ be a quadratic character on $G$, and let $u$ of the primes in $A$ satisfy $\psi(q)=1$, with $v$ satisfying $\psi(q)=-1$. Exactly $2uv$ ordered pairs can land in the negative coset, because one factor must come from each sign. If $u=0$ then every binary product has positive sign, while a ternary product of negative-sign primes has negative sign. This is an abstract obstruction model, not a claim that $u=0$ actually occurs for large prime moduli. It explains why a ternary theorem cannot simply be shortened, and why the primary paper handles exceptional quadratic concentration separately.
