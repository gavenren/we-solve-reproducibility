# Tuza's inequality on width-six graphs and sharper triangulations, with a monotonicity boundary

**Claim type: Literature report.** Tuza's exact integral inequality holds for every finite simple graph of treewidth at most six. A stronger factor \(3/2\) holds for planar triangulations other than \(K_4\). These are credited results of Botler–Fernandes–Gutiérrez. We state their exact versions and prove a concrete limitation: the stronger ratio does not transfer from a triangulation to an arbitrary planar subgraph.

The report concerns edge-disjoint triangle packing and edge transversals exactly as in the prompt, rather than vertex-disjoint triangles or fractional relaxations.

## Three checked primary theorems

In arXiv2002.07925v2:
- Theorem3.4 proves \(\tau_\triangle(G)\le2\nu_\triangle(G)\) for every graph with treewidth at most six.
- Theorem4.5 proves \(\tau_\triangle(G)\le\frac32\nu_\triangle(G)\) for every planar triangulation \(G\ne K_4\).
- Theorem5.1 proves \(\tau_\triangle(G)\le\frac95\nu_\triangle(G)+\frac15\) for every 3-tree.

A tree decomposition has bags covering all vertices and edges, with the bags containing each fixed vertex inducing a connected subtree; its width is one less than the largest bag size. A 3-tree is built from a triangle by repeatedly adding a vertex adjacent to all three vertices of an existing triangle. These restrictions are substantive and are not imposed by the original problem.

The v2 PDF's exact theorem labels are retained: its introduction points to **Theorem3.4**, not a theorem number from another version. The long local-configuration and tree-decomposition proofs are credited external inputs, not presented as newly proved here. The published paper is *Discrete Mathematics*344(4)(2021),112281, DOI10.1016/j.disc.2020.112281.

The integral rounding is automatic: the second theorem gives \(\tau\le\lfloor3\nu/2\rfloor\); the third gives \(\tau\le\lfloor(9\nu+1)/5\rfloor\). A real-valued bound does not permit rounding its right side upward to claim an improved coefficient.

## A complete boundary example inside planar triangulations

Let \(G=K_4\) on vertices \(1,2,3,4\). Every two of its four triangles share an edge, so \(\nu_\triangle(G)=1\). No single edge meets all four triangles: each edge belongs to exactly two. The pair \(\{12,34\}\) does meet all triangles. Thus
\[
 \tau_\triangle(K_4)=2,\qquad \nu_\triangle(K_4)=1.
\]
This explains the explicit exception in the factor-\(3/2\) theorem.

Now add a vertex \(x\) inside the triangular face \(123\), adjacent to \(1,2,3\), retaining all edges of \(K_4\). The result \(G^+\) is the simple planar triangulation \(K_5-4x\), with five vertices and nine edges.

The triangles \(124\) and \(13x\) are edge-disjoint, so \(\nu_\triangle(G^+)\ge2\). It cannot be three. Three edge-disjoint triangles would use all nine edges, but their union has even degree at every vertex, whereas \(4\) and \(x\) each have degree three in \(G^+\). Hence
\[
 \nu_\triangle(G^+)=2.
\]

The edge set \(\{12,13,23\}\) meets every triangle: any triangle uses at least two of \(1,2,3\), because \(4x\) is absent. Therefore \(\tau_\triangle(G^+)\le3\). A cover of size two would have to cover all \(K_4\) triangles. Both its edges would then lie in \(K_4\), and must be one of the opposite-edge pairs
\[
 \{12,34\},\quad\{13,24\},\quad\{14,23\}.
\]
An adjacent pair of edges misses the triangle on the other endpoint and the two remaining vertices, so no other two-edge pair covers \(K_4\). The three displayed pairs respectively miss \(13x,12x,12x\). Hence none covers \(G^+\), proving
\[
 \tau_\triangle(G^+)=3.
\]
Thus the triangulation has ratio \(3/2\), while its planar subgraph \(K_4\) has ratio two.

## Why triangulating first does not prove the sharper planar bound

Both \(\tau\) and \(\nu\) can increase when edges or vertices are added. The inequality \(\tau(G^+)\le c\nu(G^+)\) therefore does not imply \(\tau(G)\le c\nu(G)\): the packing gain may be crucial. The exact example shows failure already at five vertices.

This does not affect the original factor-two conjecture for planar graphs, which is established literature separately; it blocks an invalid extension of the **stronger** factor-\(3/2\) theorem. Nor does a graph being a subgraph of a width-six graph require a separate extension argument: treewidth is monotone under taking subgraphs, so the first theorem applies directly, with its own factor two.
