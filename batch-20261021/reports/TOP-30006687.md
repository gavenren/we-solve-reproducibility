# Parity games as a total dual-certificate language with a quasipolynomial algorithm

Problem: TOP-30006687  
Claim type: Literature report  
Author: /root/hundred_algorithms

**Exact scope.** The total explicitly encoded parity-game winner language is placed in NP∩coNP using checked positional-strategy certificates, and the credited Calude–Jain–Khoussainov–Li–Stephan quasipolynomial algorithm is specialized with binary priority and input-length accounting. This does not decide whether all NP∩coNP languages are in P.

A valid instance is a finite directed graph with at least one vertex, at least one outgoing edge at every vertex, a partition into Even/Odd-owned vertices, binary nonnegative priorities, and a start vertex. Even wins if the largest priority appearing infinitely often is even. Invalid encodings are rejected by definition. This makes a total language, not a promise that the supplied pair of verifiers describes a game.

Use the classical positional determinacy theorem for parity games: exactly one player has a memoryless winning strategy from every start. For completeness of the following certificates this theorem is needed; it is not inferred from finite play enumeration. A certificate for Even selects one outgoing edge at every Even vertex. Retain these edges and all Odd-owned edges, and restrict to vertices reachable from the start. It is winning exactly when there is no reachable directed cycle whose largest priority is odd. Indeed such a cycle can be reached and repeated by the opponent, since the selected Even edges on the cycle are fixed. Conversely, in any losing infinite play, the vertices occurring infinitely often form a nonempty strongly connected recurrent set; an odd vertex of its maximal priority lies on a directed cycle within that set. The cycle has that odd maximum. The same argument with players reversed verifies Odd certificates.

The cycle condition is polynomial-time checkable without listing cycles. For every odd priority p, take the induced subgraph on vertices of priority at most p, and test whether a globally reachable vertex of priority p lies on a directed cycle there. A strongly connected component of size at least two, or a singleton with a self-loop, gives such a cycle. Reachability is tested in the original retained graph: a path reaching the cycle may visit priorities larger than p finitely often, which is harmless. This detail prevents an incorrect verifier that restricts the prefix as well as the recurrent cycle. There are at most n tested priorities; standard finite graph searches and comparisons of binary priorities take poly(L) bit time in the full encoding length L.

A winning positional strategy consists of n successor indices, hence polynomially many bits. Positional determinacy guarantees at least one of the two certificate types and excludes simultaneous acceptance. Invalid encodings have an immediate deterministic complement certificate. Therefore the total language is in NP∩coNP.

**Credited algorithmic bound.** Theorem 2.7 of the checked STOC 2017 primary paper gives winner determination in n^{O(log m)} time for n vertices and m relevant priority values. Binary numerical magnitudes do not force a pseudopolynomial dependence. Sort the distinct priorities and replace them successively by a strictly increasing parity-preserving sequence: choose 0 or 1 for the first, then increase by one or two to match the next parity. The resulting largest value is at most 2m≤2n and every infinite play keeps the parity of its largest recurrent value. Sorting and replacement cost poly(L). If the theorem’s indexing uses priorities in {1,…,m} with m at most the vertex count, add two to all priorities and pad to 2n+2 vertices with unreachable self-loops; parity and the start winner are preserved and the normalization holds. Thus the theorem gives 2^{O(log²(n+2))}·poly(L) bit time, allowing polynomial overhead for encoded counters and graph access. This is quasipolynomial in L, not a polynomial whose exponent is independent of n.

The same paper obtains stronger priority-parameter bounds; no sharper exponent or fixed-parameter constant is required here. Its algorithm and positional determinacy are known literature results, explicitly credited. The complete certificate verifier above explains why this is an ordinary total two-sided-certificate example. It is distinct from the earlier factoring/permutation search examples and supplies a concrete general algorithmic partial result for one significant subclass, while leaving the class equality untouched.
