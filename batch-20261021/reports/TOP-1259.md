# TOP-1259 — Quantitative digit obstructions beyond qualitative complexity

**Claim type: Literature report.** The literal target is normality of every real algebraic irrational in every integer base. This report records two quantitative results with their different quantifiers, and proves an explicit infinite-family consequence of the digit-change theorem. It does not prove normality or exhibit an algebraic counterexample.

Let $p_\xi(n,b)$ count distinct length-$n$ blocks in the base-$b$ fractional expansion of $\xi$. Let
\[
D_\xi(N,b)=\#\{1\le j\le N:a_j\ne a_{j+1}\}.
\]
The earlier contribution already discusses the qualitative lower bound $p_\xi(n,b)/n\to\infty$, Fourier criteria, long-run restrictions and a biased random sequence. Merely repeating that discussion would add no contribution. The addition here is the stronger limsup statement and an effective digit-change lower bound, followed by a full gap-series deduction whose long-run ratios evade the older elementary obstruction.

## 1. Precisely credited quantitative inputs

Bugeaud and Evertse [1, Theorem 2.1] prove that, for algebraic irrational $0<\xi<1$, integer $b\ge2$ and every real $\eta<1/11$,
\[
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_\xi(n,b)}{n(\log n)^\eta}=\infty.
\tag{1}
\]
For positive $\eta$, this supplies arbitrarily large multiples of $n(\log n)^\eta$ along an unbounded subsequence. It is a limsup conclusion; it does not assert that this lower bound holds at every sufficiently large $n$. The qualitative divergence in the earlier contribution does not by itself specify any logarithmic rate.

Their Theorem 3.1 gives effectively computable positive constants $c_1$ and $c_2(\xi,b)$ such that, if $\xi$ has algebraic degree $d$,
\[
D_\xi(N,b)\ge
\frac{c_1(\log N)^{3/2}}
{\sqrt{\log\log N}\sqrt{\log(6d)}}
\quad(N\ge c_2(\xi,b)).
\tag{2}
\]
Here $c_1$ is absolute. Unlike (1), (2) holds at all sufficiently large integers. The degree and the threshold cannot be omitted. The source supplies effectiveness of these constants; this report does not compute them.

Both are deep external results, not proofs supplied here. The checked PDF is arXiv:0709.1560v1, dated 11 September 2007. Its arXiv record identifies the published article as Acta Arithmetica 133 (2008), 221–250, DOI 10.4064/aa133-3-3.

## 2. A complete gap-series consequence

Fix an integer $b\ge2$ and a real parameter $2/3<\delta<1$. For all sufficiently large integers $j$, put
\[
m_j=\lfloor\exp(j^\delta)\rfloor.
\]
Choose $J$ so that $m_j$ is strictly increasing and $m_{j+1}-m_j\ge2$ for $j\ge J$, and define
\[
\xi_{b,\delta}=\sum_{j\ge J}b^{-m_j}.
\tag{3}
\]
Such a $J$ exists: the derivative of $\exp(x^\delta)$ is $\delta x^{\delta-1}\exp(x^\delta)$, which tends to infinity. Enlarging $J$ also makes (3) lie in $(0,1)$. Its base-$b$ expansion has a digit $1$ at precisely the positions $m_j$ and $0$ elsewhere. Each position is used once, so no carrying is hidden in this assertion.

We claim that every number (3) is transcendental.

First, it is irrational. Its expansion has infinitely many ones, separated by unbounded strings of zeros. An eventually periodic expansion containing a nonzero digit has bounded zero-run lengths, while an expansion eventually consisting only of zeros has only finitely many ones. Both possibilities are excluded.

Next, write $T(N)=\#\{j\ge J:m_j\le N\}$. If $m_j\le N$, then $\exp(j^\delta)<N+1$, and hence
\[
T(N)\le 1+(\log(N+1))^{1/\delta}.
\]
Every isolated one contributes at most two changes. Thus
\[
D_{\xi_{b,\delta}}(N,b)\le
2+2(\log(N+2))^{1/\delta}.
\tag{4}
\]
The harmless added constants cover the first and last counting positions.

If (3) were algebraic, its already-proved irrationality would allow (2). For its fixed finite degree $d$, the ratio of the lower-bound growth in (2) to the upper-bound growth in (4) tends to infinity:
\[
\frac{(\log N)^{3/2-1/\delta}}{\sqrt{\log\log N}}\longrightarrow\infty,
\]
because $\delta>2/3$. The factors depending on $d$ and the positive absolute constant do not affect this limit. Eventually the two inequalities contradict each other. This proves the claim for every $b$ and every $\delta$ in the stated interval.

The argument is a classical-style consequence of the credited theorem, not a novelty claim. The report supplies the full verification of this parameterization.

## 3. What this says about the normality problem

The numbers (3) are visibly nonnormal: the number of nonzero digits among the first $N$ is $O((\log N)^{1/\delta})=o(N)$, so the frequency of digit $1$ is zero rather than $1/b$. Their transcendence therefore prevents this family from supplying algebraic counterexamples to the prompt.

There is also a useful boundary for the older long-run argument. Since $\delta<1$,
\[
\frac{m_{j+1}}{m_j}\to1,\qquad
\frac{m_{j+1}-m_j}{m_j}\to0.
\]
Indeed, $(j+1)^\delta-j^\delta\to0$, and flooring has relative error tending to zero. Thus the long intervening zero runs have length sublinear in their starting positions. They do not violate a restriction of the form $L\le(1+\varepsilon)m+O(1)$ on a run beginning after position $m$. The quantitative digit-change theorem nevertheless excludes algebraicity. This explains exactly why the additional input goes beyond the earlier run-length discussion.

Neither (1) nor (2) gives uniform frequencies of all blocks. Even (2) remains a polylogarithmic lower bound for a statistic that a normal number would have of linear order. We do not infer normality from a lower bound on complexity or changes, and we do not strengthen a limsup into an eventual estimate.
