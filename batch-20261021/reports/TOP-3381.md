# TOP-3381 — The square-free case, its CRT formulation, and the remaining obstruction

**Claim type: Literature report.** The prompt asks whether a finite collection of residue classes with pairwise distinct odd moduli greater than one can cover every integer. A complete answer is known when every modulus is square-free. This report identifies the precise credited theorem, explains its finite geometric interpretation and resulting uncovered arithmetic progression, and separates the prime-power obstruction that the reduction does not remove.

The result used is Balister–Bollobás–Morris–Sahasrabudhe–Tiba [1, Theorem 1.1]: a finite covering by residue classes with distinct square-free moduli greater than one has an even modulus. Its square-free hypothesis is essential to the stated scope. The theorem does not settle the original question for arbitrary odd moduli.

A second result of the same authors [2, Theorem 1.4] supplies an independent necessary condition for any distinct covering. If $Q$ is the least common multiple of the moduli, then
\[
2\mid Q\quad\hbox{or}\quad9\mid Q\quad\hbox{or}\quad15\mid Q.
\tag{1}
\]
In a putative odd covering, the first possibility is absent. The last possibility concerns the period $Q$: it need not arise from one modulus divisible by fifteen. The source explicitly distinguishes the possibility that a modulus divisible by three and another divisible by five provide the two prime factors.

Both are external sieve theorems and are credited as such. The deductions below do not purport to reproduce their distortion-sieve proofs.

## 1. The exact finite translation for square-free odd moduli

Take any finite family $a_i+q_i\mathbb Z$ with all $q_i>1$, odd, square-free and pairwise unequal. Let $Q=\operatorname{lcm}(q_1,\ldots,q_k)$. Membership in its union depends only on the residue modulo $Q$, so the original infinite covering problem is equivalent to covering the finite ring $\mathbb Z/Q\mathbb Z$.

Write the distinct primes in $Q$ as $p_1,\ldots,p_r$. The Chinese remainder map is a bijection
\[
\mathbb Z/Q\mathbb Z\longrightarrow\prod_{j=1}^r\mathbb Z/p_j\mathbb Z.
\]
For a fixed modulus $q_i$, its residue class requires the coordinate modulo $p_j$ to equal $a_i\bmod p_j$ whenever $p_j\mid q_i$, and imposes no condition at the other coordinates. Hence its image is a proper coordinate hyperplane: a product with each factor either a singleton or the whole coordinate set, and with at least one fixed coordinate because $q_i>1$.

The fixed-coordinate support is exactly the set of prime divisors of $q_i$. Because the moduli are square-free, different moduli have different supports. Therefore no two resulting hyperplanes are parallel in the source's terminology.

The geometric result [1, Theorem 1.2] says that the product whose coordinate sizes are the consecutive odd primes cannot be covered by proper hyperplanes with pairwise different nonempty fixed-coordinate supports. If some odd primes below the largest divisor of $Q$ are absent from $Q$, add those coordinates freely to every hyperplane. Covering the enlarged box would be equivalent to covering the original box, and distinct nonempty supports remain distinct. Thus the geometric theorem applies without an unstated assumption that the prime divisors of $Q$ themselves are consecutive.

It follows that the union misses a residue $c\bmod Q$. Every integer in the entire progression $c+Q\mathbb Z$ is then uncovered.

This last statement is a useful exact strengthening of the bare wording “does not cover”: for every family in this infinite square-free class, the uncovered set is infinite and has natural density at least $1/Q$. The bound depends on the actual period. No uniform positive lower bound independent of the moduli is asserted.

## 2. Why taking radicals does not settle arbitrary odd moduli

For a general modulus $q$, replacing $a+q\mathbb Z$ by $a+\operatorname{rad}(q)\mathbb Z$ enlarges the residue class, since $\operatorname{rad}(q)\mid q$. A cover would therefore remain a cover after this replacement.

However, distinctness need not survive. For example, the distinct odd moduli $3$ and $9$ both have radical $3$. The two resulting classes may have the same modulus, which falls outside the square-free distinct-modulus theorem. Their residues may differ, so one cannot simply discard a duplicate modulus while preserving a cover. In geometric language, unequal prime-power moduli can impose restrictions having the same prime-coordinate support.

Consequently the following tempting argument is invalid: enlarge every progression to its radical, apply the square-free theorem, and conclude that no odd covering exists. The enlargement removes exactly the distinctness information required by the cited theorem.

## 3. Restrictions on any remaining counterexample

Combining the square-free result with (1), any counterexample to the prompt must satisfy all of the following:

* every modulus is odd and greater than one, with no repetition;
* at least one modulus contains a repeated prime factor;
* its period is divisible by nine or by fifteen.

In particular, if nine does not divide the period, the period must contain both three and five. This need not force a modulus divisible by fifteen. Also, the existence of some square factor cannot alone produce a cover: these are necessary conditions, not a construction or sufficiency theorem.

The report resolves the full infinite class of distinct odd square-free families by attribution to [1], and locates the precise defect in extending that result to arbitrary prime powers. It does not claim that the remaining possibilities occur.
