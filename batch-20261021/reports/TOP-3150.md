# Reconstructing all spiders through a top-leaf polynomial and divisor inversion

**Claim type: Literature report.** Every spider is determined, among all finite trees, by its chromatic symmetric function. Here a spider has exactly one vertex of degree at least three, with legs of arbitrary positive lengths. This is an established theorem of Martin–Morin–Wagner. We give a complete reconstruction using the highest leaf-edge coefficient and a divisor recurrence, after explicitly stating their external subtree-polynomial theorem.

The result extends far beyond stars and double-stars but does not settle the question for every tree.

## The credited bridge from the symmetric function

For a tree \(T\), let
\[
 S_T(q,r)=\sum_U q^{|E(U)|}r^{\ell(U)},
\]
where \(U\) runs over its nonempty-edge subtrees and \(\ell(U)\) is the number of **leaf edges** of \(U\). For a single edge \(\ell(U)=1\); for a tree with at least two edges, it equals the number of degree-one vertices.

Martin–Morin–Wagner, arXivmath/0609339v3, Theorem1, proves that \(X_T\) determines \(S_T\) by a fixed linear transformation of its power-sum coefficients. Their Corollary5 recovers degrees, and Theorem8 reconstructs spiders. We use the first theorem as external input and give the following explicit degree recognition and reconstruction in full. No claim that the entire transformation theorem is proved anew is made.

## Recognizing the spider among all trees

For \(k\ge2\), let \(\sigma_k=[q^kr^k]S_T\). A \(k\)-edge tree whose every edge is a leaf edge is a star. Conversely every \(k\)-edge star has \(k\) leaf edges. Its center is unique for \(k\ge2\), so, writing \(\delta_j\) for the number of vertices of degree \(j\),
\[
 \sigma_k=\sum_{j\ge k}\binom jk\delta_j. \tag{1}
\]
Hence descending binomial inversion gives, for \(j\ge2\),
\[
 \delta_j=\sum_{k\ge j}(-1)^{k-j}\binom kj\sigma_k. \tag{2}
\]
For completeness, the coefficient of \(\delta_t\) after substitution is
\[
 \sum_{k=j}^t(-1)^{k-j}\binom kj\binom tk
 =\binom tj(1-1)^{t-j},
\]
which is one at \(t=j\) and zero otherwise.

The order \(n\) is the homogeneous degree of \(X_T\). For \(n\ge2\), a tree has no degree-zero vertex, so \(\delta_1=n-\sum_{j\ge2}\delta_j\). The one-vertex tree is handled directly. Thus \(X_T\) determines the entire degree sequence and, in particular, whether exactly one vertex has degree at least three.

If so, call its degree \(m\ge3\). Deleting that vertex leaves \(m\) paths, because all other vertices have degree at most two and the graph is a tree. Their lengths from the center to their terminal leaves form an unordered positive multiset \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m\). This multiset determines the whole tree up to isomorphism.

## A product recording all leg lengths

Let \(Q(q)=[r^m]S_T(q,r)\). A subtree avoiding the center, or using fewer than all \(m\) legs, has fewer than \(m\) leaf edges: it is either a path or a smaller spider. A subtree with exactly \(m\) leaf edges must contain the center and a nonempty initial segment of every leg. Conversely each independent choice of a prefix of length \(a_i\in\{1,\ldots,\lambda_i\}\) gives such a subtree. Therefore
\[
 Q(q)=\prod_{i=1}^m(q+q^2+\cdots+q^{\lambda_i}). \tag{3}
\]
In particular \(q^m\) divides \(Q\), and
\[
 P(q)={(1-q)^m Q(q)\over q^m}
     =\prod_{i=1}^m(1-q^{\lambda_i}) \tag{4}
\]
is completely determined by \(X_T\). It has constant coefficient one, so its inverse exists as a formal power series.

Let \(a_d=\#\{i:\lambda_i=d\}\), and define \(c_s\) as the coefficient of \(q^s\) in \(-qP'(q)/P(q)\). Taking the logarithmic derivative of the finite product (4) gives the formal identity
\[
 -{qP'(q)\over P(q)}
  =\sum_{d\ge1}a_d\,{dq^d\over1-q^d},
 \qquad
 c_s=\sum_{d\mid s}d\,a_d. \tag{5}
\]
Thus the lengths can be recovered successively by the explicit recurrence
\[
 a_s={1\over s}
 \left(c_s-\sum_{\substack{d\mid s\\d<s}}d\,a_d\right). \tag{6}
\]
Everything is exact rational polynomial or power-series arithmetic, with no analytic convergence needed. Computing through \(s=n-1\) suffices, since no leg is longer than the tree's \(n-1\) edges. The data come from an actual spider, so the recovered values are automatically nonnegative integers; no such assertion is made for arbitrary input polynomials.

If another tree \(T'\) has the same \(X\), equations(1)–(2) force it to be a spider with the same \(m\). It then has the same \(Q,P,c_s\), and (6) gives the same leg multiset. Therefore \(T'\cong T\). This proves determination among **all trees**, rather than merely injectivity after assuming both inputs belong to a supplied list of spiders.

## Relation to the earlier manuscript and the remaining boundary

Math-1063-A1 gives the power-sum cut interpretation, reconstructs paths, stars and double-stars, and exhibits two trees whose single-edge-cut profiles agree. Its example warns correctly that a two-part projection alone need not reconstruct a general tree. Here the credited subtree polynomial and its top-leaf coefficient recover every spider, including arbitrarily unequal and arbitrarily numerous nontrivial legs.

Neither (3) nor (6) applies to a tree with several branching vertices: an \(m\)-leaf subtree need not be independent prefixes of a single center's legs. Nor does the external theorem claim the subtree polynomial determines every tree; the primary paper explicitly provides collisions for that weaker invariant. No unrestricted conclusion is drawn.

This source-only report supplies a complete reconstruction after the cited external bridge. No computational enumeration, current-best claim or novelty claim is made.
