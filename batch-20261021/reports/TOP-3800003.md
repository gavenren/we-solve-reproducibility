# An explicit all-integer lower bound for nonsimplicial facets

The credited existence input is the Joswig–Ziegler neighborly-cubical theorem: for integers$m\ge d\ge2r+2$, there is a convex cubical$d$-polytope whose$r$-skeleton is that of the$m$-cube. Set$d=4,r=1$. Its vertex and edge counts are
\[
v=2^m,\qquad e=m2^{m-1}.
\]
Every facet is a combinatorial3-cube, so all its facets are nonsimplicial under the prompt's definition. We derive the exact facet count and extend the construction to every sufficiently large vertex count:
\[
D_4(2^m)\ge(m-2)2^{m-2},\qquad
D_4(n)\ge\frac{n(\log_2 n-3)}8\quad(n\ge16).
\]
These are lower bounds, not sharp asymptotics or new realizations of the source's polytopes.

Write$f=f_3$ and$s=f_2$. Each cubical facet has six square ridges, and every ridge of a convex4-polytope belongs to exactly two facets, giving$s=3f$. Euler's relation$v-e+s-f=0$ therefore gives$f=(e-v)/2=(m-2)2^{m-2}$. In particular the nonsimplicial-facet-to-vertex ratio is$(m-2)/4$, growing logarithmically. This makes the prompt's already-resolved threshold$D_4(n)\ge2n$ quantitatively explicit; the credited existence theorem, rather than abstract face counts alone, guarantees convex realizability.

The following elementary stacking observation removes the power-of-two restriction. Choose a point just beyond the relative interior of a facet$F$ and strictly beneath every other supporting facet hyperplane. The convex hull with that point replaces$F$ by pyramids over the ridges of$F$, preserves all old vertices, and adds one vertex. To justify the position, begin at a relative-interior point of$F$, where every other facet inequality is strict, and move a sufficiently short distance outward normal to$F$. The new boundary consists of the old invisible facets and cones over the visible facet's boundary; no other facet is visible.

First stack on a cubical facet. Its six square ridges produce six square pyramids, each with five vertices and hence nonsimplicial as a3-dimensional facet. Thus one nonsimplicial facet is removed and six are added. Further stacking on a square-pyramid facet removes that facet and replaces it with one square pyramid over its square base and four tetrahedra over its triangular sides. The nonsimplicial-facet count is unchanged, and another square pyramid remains available for the next step. Arbitrarily many individual extra vertices can therefore be added without decreasing the initial count.

For$2^m\le n<2^{m+1}$, pad the$m$-cube-skeleton example in this way. Since$2^m>n/2$ and$m>\log_2n-1$, its count is at least$n(\log_2n-3)/8$ for$n\ge16$. The weak displayed inequality also holds at powers of two by the stronger exact count.
