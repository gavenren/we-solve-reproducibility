# A falling-factorial cycle count with separate chromatic and modulus parameters

**Claim type: Literature report.** For every \(k\ge3\), the prompt's conclusion holds whenever \(\chi(G)\ge k+3\), with the stronger count
\[
 c_{0,k}(G)\ge (k+1)!/4.
 \tag{1}
\]
We prove this using the generalized-color-permutation argument of Moore and West [1, Theorem4]. The unsolved boundary within this argument is precisely the two possible chromatic levels \(k+1,k+2\), not all high chromatic numbers. No new-priority claim is made.

Here is the more general statement that keeps the parameters distinct. Let \(3\le r\le K\), and suppose \(F\) is not \(K\)-colorable but \(F-e\) is \(K\)-colorable for some edge \(e=xy\). Then \(F-e\) contains at least
\[
 \frac12\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}(K-i)
 \tag{2}
\]
distinct undirected simple cycles of lengths divisible by \(r\).

Fix a proper \(K\)-coloring \(\phi\) of \(F-e\). Necessarily \(\phi(x)=\phi(y)=c\), since otherwise it colors \(F\). Choose a cyclic permutation \(\sigma\) on any \(r\) colors including \(c\). There are \((K-1)(K-2)\cdots(K-r+1)\) such choices, because the ordered colors following \(c\) determine \(\sigma\). Define a digraph \(D_\sigma\) on vertices whose colors lie in this selected set, orienting an edge \(uv\) from \(u\) to \(v\) when \(\phi(v)=\sigma(\phi(u))\). Since \(r\ge3\), an edge never receives both orientations.

If \(D_\sigma\) were acyclic, choose sinks successively and recolor each chosen vertex from \(\phi(v)\) to \(\sigma(\phi(v))\), using a sink of the still unchanged subdigraph at every step. At a recoloring, an unchanged neighbor could acquire equal color only if it is an outgoing neighbor in the unchanged digraph, contrary to the sink choice. A previously changed neighbor cannot have equal new color because \(\sigma\) is injective and the original coloring was proper. Neighbors whose colors are outside the selected set also cause no conflict. Thus every intermediate coloring remains proper on \(F-e\).

At the first recoloring of either \(x\) or \(y\), these two endpoints receive different colors, as one changes from \(c\) to \(\sigma(c)\) and the other is still unchanged. The intermediate coloring would therefore color all of \(F\), a contradiction. Hence \(D_\sigma\) contains a directed simple cycle. Its colors advance around the \(r\)-cycle \(\sigma\), so its length is a positive multiple of \(r\).

For fixed \(\phi\), an undirected cycle selected this way determines \(\sigma\) up to reversal: either direction around it specifies the successor of every selected color, and all \(r\) colors appear. A permutation and its inverse may give the same undirected cycle, but no third permutation can. Dividing by2 gives(2). Cycles are counted as subgraphs, exactly as in the prompt; neither starting points nor directions produce extra counts.

To deduce(1), take \(K=k+2\). From any graph with \(\chi(G)\ge K+1\), choose an inclusion-minimal non-\(K\)-colorable subgraph \(F\). It has an edge, and deletion of any edge makes it \(K\)-colorable. Apply(2) with \(r=k\). The product is
\[
 \tfrac12(K-1)(K-2)\cdots(K-k+1)
 =\tfrac12 (k+1)!/2=(k+1)!/4.
\]
Since
\[
 \frac{(k+1)!/4}{(k+1)(k-1)!/2}=k/2\ge3/2,
\]
this exceeds the requested count. Inequalities with noninteger right sides mean their ceilings, because cycle counts are integers.

Using \(K=k\) in(2) only gives \((k-1)!/2\), a factor \(k+1\) below the prompt. Using \(K=k+1\) gives \(k!/2\), still below the target. Thus one must not claim that the general falling-factorial theorem resolves \(\chi>k\) merely by confusing the coloring parameter with the modulus. The earlier contribution's critical reduction and join examples do not supply this all-\(k\), higher-chromatic regime.
