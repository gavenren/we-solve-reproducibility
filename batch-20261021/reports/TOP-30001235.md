# Characteristic-free lex-plus-powers for every monomial regular sequence

Claim type: **Literature report**.

The entire assertion in the prompt holds over **every field**, in every number of variables and every regular-sequence length, provided the given regular sequence consists of monomials. The ideal$I$ itself may be arbitrary homogeneous, not monomial. This is the published theorem of Mermin–Murai [1, Theorem8.1]; it is more than a check of the pure-power ideal alone. We record the exact normalization and the reduction that explains its scope.

Let $F=(f_1,\ldots,f_r)$ be a regular sequence of monomials with degrees $2\le e_1\le\cdots\le e_r$, and let $I\supseteq F$. Put $P=(x_1^{e_1},\ldots,x_r^{e_r})$. There is a lex-plus-$P$ ideal$L$ with
\[
 H_{S/I}=H_{S/L},\qquad
 \beta^S_{i,j}(S/I)\le\beta^S_{i,j}(S/L)\quad\text{for all }i,j. \tag{1}
\]
No characteristic-zero assumption or full-length $r=n$ assumption is needed. Variables beyond$r$ are unrestricted, so this includes non-Artinian quotients.

A monomial sequence is regular exactly when its members have pairwise disjoint variable supports. The forward implication follows because a shared variable would make a later monomial a zero divisor modulo an earlier one after multiplying by the earlier monomial divided by a common variable. For the reverse implication, if$f_jh$ belongs to the ideal of earlier monomials, divisibility of some monomial term by a previous$f_i$ forces that same divisibility in$h$, since$f_i$ and$f_j$ have disjoint supports; this is the non-zero-divisor condition. In particular each$f_i$ can be assigned a distinct variable from its support.

Here is the characteristic-free degeneration in [1, Lemma3.2]. After renaming variables select$x_i\in\operatorname{supp}f_i$. In the support of$f_i$, replace each other variable$x_k$ by$x_i+x_k$, leaving$x_i$ and all variables outside that support fixed. This is an invertible linear change over every field. In degree$e_i$, with the selected variable ordered first and graded reverse lexicographic order, the transformed$f_i$ has leading term $x_i^{e_i}$ with coefficient1. Disjoint supports leave every other$f_j$ unchanged. Take the initial ideal; it contains that pure power and the other$f_j$. Initial degeneration preserves the Hilbert function and does not decrease the graded Betti numbers. Repeating over the disjoint supports produces a monomial ideal$J\supseteq P$ with the same Hilbert function as$I$ and componentwise larger Betti numbers. The flat-degeneration Betti inequality is the standard input used by the source, not a claim that arbitrary linear changes preserve the original ideal literally.

The hard remaining theorem is the characteristic-free compression/shifting construction taking such$J$ to a Borel-plus-$P$ ideal without decreasing any graded Betti number [1, §§4–8, Theorem8.1]. The Borel-plus-$P$ comparison then gives$L$. We credit that construction, rather than substituting a generic-initial-ideal argument that would require characteristic zero. The source gives a separate shorter characteristic-zero proof in §3 and explicitly a different characteristic-free proof thereafter.

The source often states Betti inequalities for the **ideals**. To obtain the prompt’s quotient convention, use the minimal resolution of $0\to I\to S\to S/I\to0$: for a proper homogeneous ideal,
\[
 \beta_{i,j}(S/I)=\beta_{i-1,j}(I)\quad(i\ge1),
 \qquad\beta_{0,j}(S/I)=\begin{cases}1&j=0,\\0&j\ne0.\end{cases}
\]
Thus the ideal inequalities imply every quotient inequality in(1), including$i=0$. If$I=S$, take$L=S$; both quotients vanish. This avoids an index-shift gap.

Uniqueness concerns the **resulting lex-plus-powers ideal**, not a choice of an auxiliary lex ideal whose sum with$P$ presents it. In degree$d$, the standard monomials outside$P$ are fixed. The Hilbert function prescribes exactly how many of them belong to$L$, and the lex-plus-$P$ condition selects the lexicographically largest that many. Hence each$L_d$ is uniquely prescribed. The theorem guarantees these degreewise spaces form an ideal; that compatibility is not automatic for an arbitrary sequence of integers. Once existence is known, uniqueness follows immediately in every degree.

It does not prove the assertion for arbitrary homogeneous nonmonomial regular sequences or verify those claimed generalizations.
