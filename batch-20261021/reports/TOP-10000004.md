# The sharp bound is120 when reflections are allowed

The proposed bound60 fails for the literal definition, which allows all isometries of the induced Euclidean metric. The sharp bound is
\[
B=120.
\]
The distinction from orientation-preserving symmetry is essential. We give an upper bound without assuming a classification of finite orthogonal groups, and then an attaining orbit avoiding both exceptional circle configurations.

If the affine span of$X$ has dimension at most two, it lies on a circle cut out by an affine plane, hence in a union of two parallel circles. Exclude that case. An isometry of a full-affine-span finite set extends uniquely to a Euclidean isometry of$\mathbb R^3$: choose four affinely independent points, extend their distance-preserving correspondence, and use distances to those four points to determine every other image. The unique sphere through those four points is the unit sphere, so the extension fixes its center and is orthogonal. Quotienting any ineffective action gives a faithful finite group$G\subset O(3)$ transitive on$X$. Put$H=G\cap SO(3)$, of index at most two.

Here is the needed rotation-group bound. Let$N=|H|>1$, and collect the poles fixed by nonidentity rotations of$H$. At a pole the stabilizer is a cyclic rotation group of order$m\ge2$. If its pole orbits have stabilizer orders$m_1,\ldots,m_k$, counting pairs of a nonidentity rotation and one of its two fixed poles gives
\[
2(N-1)=\sum_j\frac{N}{m_j}(m_j-1),\qquad
\sum_j(1-1/m_j)=2-2/N.
\]
Consequently$k\le3$ and$k\ge2$. For$k=2$, the reciprocal equation and$m_j\le N$ force$m_1=m_2=N$; all rotations have one common axis. For$k=3$, order the$m_j$. Their reciprocal sum is$1+2/N>1$, so$m_1=2$. If$m_2\ge4$ the sum is at most one, impossible. If$m_2=3$, necessarily$m_3=3,4,$ or$5$, and the equation gives$N=12,24,$ or$60$. If$m_2=2$, it gives$N=2m_3$. For$m_3>2$ there is a unique pole orbit with stabilizer order greater than two, of size two. Its poles are antipodal, because negation preserves pole stabilizers and that orbit is unique. Hence$H$ preserves their axis. The case$m_3=2$ has$N=4$.

Thus either$N\le60$, or$H$ has a unique distinguished axis (common axis in the cyclic case, or the unique high-order pole pair in the other case). Since$H$ is normal in$G$, the latter axis is preserved as an unoriented line by$G$. A$G$-orbit on the sphere then lies in the two parallel planes$u\cdot x=\pm c$, contradicting the nonexceptional hypothesis. If$H$ is trivial,$|G|\le2$ directly. In every remaining case$|X|\le|G|\le2|H|\le120$.

For sharpness take the standard regular icosahedron with vertices given by cyclic coordinate permutations of
\[
(0,\pm1,\pm\varphi),\qquad \varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2.
\]
Its rotation group$I$ has order60: it is transitive on the12 vertices and a vertex stabilizer has order five. For completeness, cyclic coordinate permutations and changes of an even number of signs already act transitively on those vertices; the five neighbors of a vertex form a regular pentagon in a perpendicular plane. The12 vertices consist of that vertex, its antipode, its five neighbors and their negatives; hence the72-degree rotation preserves the entire set and gives the five rotations of its stabilizer. The full group$G=I\times\{1,-1\}$ has order120. Choose a unit vector$p$ outside the finitely many fixed subspaces of nonidentity elements of$G$, and with all coordinates nonzero and pairwise different in absolute value. Such vectors exist because a finite union of proper linear subspaces and the additional coordinate-equality planes cannot cover the sphere. Then$X=Gp$ has exactly120 points and is symmetric.

It remains to prove that this orbit cannot hide in two parallel circles of some other direction. It is centrally symmetric and spans three dimensions: the four even-sign-change images of$p$ span$\mathbb R^3$ whenever all$p_i\ne0$. If$u\cdot X$ had at most two values, symmetry forces those values to be$\{c,-c\}$, with$c>0$. In particular all$(u\cdot gp)^2$ would coincide. The group contains the identity and the three even sign changes. Writing$a=u_1p_1,b=u_2p_2,c'=u_3p_3$, equality of the four squared sums$a+b+c'$, $a-b-c'$, $-a+b-c'$, $-a-b+c'$ implies$ab=ac'=bc'=0$ by adding and subtracting the equations. Since every$p_i\ne0$, at most one$u_i$ is nonzero. But the group also contains cyclic coordinate permutation. Along such a coordinate axis the squared projections of the three cyclic images of$p$ are$p_1^2,p_2^2,p_3^2$, which are distinct. This is a contradiction. A great-circle containment is likewise impossible.
