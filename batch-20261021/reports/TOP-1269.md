# TOP-1269 — A quantified separation between the two Waring functions

**Claim type: Literature report.** The prompt asks for both the universal function $g(k)$ and the eventual function $G(k)$, with nonnegative integral summands. This report gives a self-contained obstruction for every exponent, an infinite fourth-power obstruction, and their precise comparison with credited upper bounds. It does not determine the two functions for all exponents.

For every integer $k\ge2$, put $q=\lfloor(3/2)^k\rfloor$. Then

\[
g(k)\ge 2^k+q-2.
\tag{1}
\]

For fourth powers, the obstruction can be made infinite: every number

\[
31\cdot16^a\qquad(a=0,1,2,\ldots)
\tag{2}
\]

requires at least sixteen nonnegative fourth powers. Consequently $G(4)\ge16$, whereas (1) gives $g(4)\ge19$. The classical upper bound $G(4)\le16$ is Davenport's theorem, credited explicitly in Brüdern and Wooley's author-hosted paper [2, introductory discussion after Theorem 1.3]. Thus the known exact value $G(4)=16$ cannot be substituted for $g(4)$.

The general upper bound used for comparison is Brüdern–Wooley [1, Theorem 1.1]:

\[
G(k)\le\left\lceil k(\log k+4.20032)\right\rceil.
\tag{3}
\]

This is a credited theorem, not a proof supplied by this report. The checked version is arXiv:2211.10380v1, dated 18 November 2022; its positive-power wording permits padding with zero terms and therefore gives the prompt's nonnegative-power conclusion. No effective value of the sufficiently-large cutoff is extracted here.

## 1. A uniform obstruction for the universal function

Let $N=q2^k-1$. Since $q2^k\le3^k$, we have $N<3^k$. Any nonnegative $k$-th power occurring in a representation of $N$ must therefore be $0,1$, or $2^k$. If there are $r$ terms equal to $2^k$, then $r\le q-1$, and exactly $N-r2^k$ positive unit terms are required. The number of positive summands is

\[
r+(N-r2^k)=N-r(2^k-1)
\ge N-(q-1)(2^k-1)=2^k+q-2.
\]

Equality is achieved for this particular $N$ by taking $q-1$ copies of $2^k$ and $2^k-1$ copies of $1$. Hence the minimum summand count of the witness $N$ is known exactly, proving (1). Zero padding changes neither the lower bound nor the witness's minimum positive count.

For $k=4$, $q=5$ and $N=79$. Its optimal representation uses four copies of $16$ and fifteen copies of $1$, so it needs nineteen terms. The fact that $79$ is only one integer would not establish a lower bound of nineteen for $G(4)$: that function explicitly tolerates finitely many exceptions.

## 2. Turning a fourth-power obstruction into infinitely many exceptions

Every fourth power is $0$ or $1\pmod {16}$, according as its base is even or odd. Suppose a positive multiple of $16$ were a sum of at most fifteen fourth powers. Pad with zeros to obtain fifteen terms. The number of odd bases would be a multiple of sixteen between zero and fifteen, so it is zero. Every base is even. Dividing the equation by sixteen produces a representation of the quotient by at most fifteen fourth powers.

Apply this descent to a hypothetical representation of $31\cdot16^a$. After $a$ divisions it would represent $31$ with at most fifteen fourth powers. Modulo sixteen, all fifteen padded bases must now be odd. But an odd fourth power not exceeding $31$ is necessarily $1$, since the next is $3^4=81$. The sum would therefore be $15$, a contradiction.

This argument proves (2) for every $a\ge0$; no extrapolation from a finite search is involved. In particular, infinitely many arbitrarily large numbers fail the fifteen-term assertion. This is exactly the quantifier needed to infer $G(4)\ge16$. It is compatible with Davenport's eventual sixteen-term theorem and with the exceptional universal witness $79$.

## 3. Why the two general tasks cannot be conflated

Combining the elementary bound (1) with the external theorem (3) yields

\[
\frac{g(k)}{G(k)}\ge
\frac{2^k+\lfloor(3/2)^k\rfloor-2}
{\lceil k(\log k+4.20032)\rceil}\longrightarrow\infty.
\]

The inference uses the upper bound for the positive denominator $G(k)$, not a conjectural formula for it. Exponential growth of $2^k$ dominates $k\log k$. Thus, across exponents, allowing finitely many exceptional integers changes the representation problem by an unbounded multiplicative factor. Determining a conjectural formula for $g(k)$ cannot settle the prompt's separate request for $G(k)$.

These bounds also identify two different proof obligations. A candidate universal upper bound must cover numbers such as $q2^k-1$; a candidate eventual upper bound need only handle all numbers beyond an exponent-dependent threshold. Conversely, a lower bound for $G(k)$ needs an unbounded family such as (2), rather than one difficult small integer.
